## 1. Which doors are realistically open to this author, and what are the odds by tier?

### Takeaway
At Nature, Comment, World View and Opinion are commission-only. The open doors are a Correspondence of under 200 words and an unsolicited Perspective, which needs a synopsis first and is peer-reviewed. At Science, the open doors are a 300-word Letter and the 720-word editorial page, where "we don't take most of what we get". In the Nature portfolio, Nature Human Behaviour, Nature Machine Intelligence and Nature Computational Science list Comment and Perspective as submittable types, and these are the realistic Nature-branded doors. In the life sciences, PLOS Biology Essays, EMBO Reports Science & Society and eLife Features take unsolicited proposals. No venue publishes acceptance rates for opinion formats. The only verified overall figures are Nature's "about 8%" and Science's 5.3%, the latter from a search summary.

Comment pieces "road-map a proposed solution in detail; they do not simply snapshot a problem." — [Nature: other submissions](https://www.nature.com/nature/for-authors/other-subs)
- News and Comment "are written and commissioned by Nature's editors. They do not contain unsolicited material. Information for use by Nature for these sections can be sent via e-mail". — [Nature: other submissions](https://www.nature.com/nature/for-authors/other-subs)

### Inferences
- **Tier 1 (Nature/Science flagship).** Odds are **very low** for Nature Perspective, Science editorial and Science Policy Forum. Odds are **low** to **low–medium** for Nature Correspondence and a Science Letter. The Correspondence door is real and busy, and single authors from many institutions get through. Its acceptance rate is unknown.
- **Tier 2 (Nature-portfolio Perspective or Comment at NHB, NMI or NCS).** Odds are **low** to **low–medium**. These formats take unsolicited work, the theme fits, and an EMBL affiliation reads as a serious researcher. But editors there have already published related pieces in 2026, and Perspectives face full peer review by metascience or social-science specialists.
- **Tier 3 (PLOS Biology Essay, EMBO Reports Science & Society, eLife Feature).** Odds are **medium**. These venues explicitly publish working scientists on research culture. The author's community reads them. EMBO Reports, with a 5-day median first decision and editor-led review, probably gives the best odds per week spent.
- **Tier 4 (specialist journals: Minerva, Research Evaluation, SPP, PSE).** Odds are **medium**, sometimes higher, but reach is low (see Q3).
- Desk decisions are fast across tiers 1–3: about 1 week at Nature, 6 days median at NHB, 5 at EMBO Reports and about 6 on average at PLOS Biology. Aiming high therefore costs little time unless a manuscript is rejected after full review.

### Gaps
- No venue publishes acceptance rates for Correspondence, Comment, Perspective or Essay formats. The Science figures come from a search summary and their data year is unclear.
- Science Policy Forum and Perspective rules, PNAS Opinion fees, and Science and PNAS AI-policy pages could not be fetched directly because of Cloudflare.
- Nature World View examples were not enumerated, because the article-type filter returned nothing.
- Nature Careers column length and format were not checked. The section only says it "welcomes suggestions".

## 2. Who wrote comparable high-profile pieces in 2023–2026, and what made them land?

### Takeaway
The high-reach pieces in this literature were written by established academics or teams. Several of those authors sit outside metascience proper (psychiatry, linguistics, computer science), but none was a single-author normative essay by an outsider in a flagship opinion slot. Pieces by outsiders landed when they brought **new data** (Hanson et al.; Fire & Guestrin) or **perfect timing** (van Dis et al., February 2023). The 2026 space is crowded and attention per piece has fallen sharply. Two 2026 journal articles, Manabu (PSE) and Hansen (RIPR), argue close to the author's thesis.

### Inferences
- **The attention premium has decayed.** The first-wave Comment (February 2023) reached 192k accesses. 2025–26 Comments on adjacent themes reach 6k–19k, and Correspondences reach 1k–6k. Being "about AI and science" is no longer enough to draw attention. A specific new angle or new data is needed.
- **Nature returns to authors it has already published on the topic.** Messeri & Crockett went from a 2024 Perspective to a 2026 Comment. An unknown single author is unlikely to be commissioned for a Comment.
- **What landed for authors outside the field was data.** Hanson et al. and Fire & Guestrin both brought new analyses. The author's equivalent is the paper's own production record: time, cost and agent logs. Without those figures, the "paper as its own exhibit" claim is just an anecdote.
- **The thesis must be differentiated** from Braun (accountability), Messeri & Crockett (narrowing, judgement and training), Manabu (the end of publish-or-perish) and Hansen (symbolic scarcity). The distinctive claim is that **even accountable, disclosed, well-done AI use breaks scarcity-based signals**. Braun's standfirst makes that claim a natural reply.

### Gaps
- No single-author, non-specialist normative essay was found in a Nature or Science flagship opinion slot in 2023–2026. The search was not exhaustive, so treat this as an absence of evidence.
- Career stages of the Correspondence authors were not verified.
- The Correspondences by Hutchinson and Motoki are paywalled, so their arguments were not read.
- Media coverage of Hanson et al.'s preprint stage was not checked.

## 3. What does "impact" look like per venue, and how do prestige and reach differ?

### Takeaway
Impact has four components, and they do not move together:
- **citations**, which follow JIF tier and peer-reviewed status;
- **reach among scientists**, which follows open access, readership and the author's own community;
- **media and policy pickup**, which follows brand and press offices;
- **influence on assessment bodies**, which follows the specialist readership.

Prestige (Nature brand, NHB's JIF of 17.5) does not equal reach. NHB opinion pieces are subscription-only, and Nature Correspondences reach 1–6k readers. OA life-science essays (PLOS Biology, EMBO Reports) trade prestige for openness and audience. Specialist journals (Minerva JIF 2.9, Research Evaluation 2.7) reach the people who run assessment, but few others.

### Inferences
- **Citations**: a peer-reviewed Perspective or Essay will be cited far more than a Comment or Correspondence. Within this literature the best-cited items are Perspectives and analyses (Messeri & Crockett 683; Hanson 293; Smaldino & McElreath 853), plus first-wave Comments.
- **Reach among scientists**: for a life scientist at EMBL, PLOS Biology and EMBO Reports are where peers read. Both are OA and free to publish. NHB adds prestige but sits behind a paywall, though Nature's SharedIt view-only links soften this.
- **Policymakers and assessment bodies**: the Nature brand and NHB carry furthest in media and policy. Research Evaluation, Minerva and SPP are read by the professionals who design evaluation (ANECA, CoARA). That is a small but decision-relevant audience.
- **Media**: the "openly AI-made paper" hook plus the EMBL brand is the strongest media lever, whatever the venue. A Nature-family venue amplifies it.

## 4. How should the work be sequenced: preprints, two tracks, special issues, conferences and media?

### Takeaway
Preprint options have narrowed sharply in the past year:
- arXiv's computer-science categories require peer review first for position papers.
- SocArXiv's AI policy rejects verbatim AI-generated paragraphs and "superficial ... theory synthesis".
- bioRxiv excludes essays.
- Zenodo accepts the work. MetaArXiv's position is unknown.

The Nature Correspondence's dual-publication clause means a preprint should wait until the Correspondence is decided (≤3 weeks). No open special issue fits well, so do not chase one. For conferences, Metascience 2027 (Montreal, 14–16 June 2027) is the best fit. On media, Nature-portfolio policy allows answering journalists about a preprint but "strongly discourage[s]" soliciting coverage before publication.

### Cited Findings
**Preprints**
- **arXiv, 31 Oct 2025**: "review articles and position papers must now be accepted at a journal or a conference and complete successful peer review" before submission to the CS category. Science-and-society *research* papers in cs.CY or physics.soc-ph "are not subject to this moderation practice change". — [arXiv blog](https://blog.arxiv.org/2025/10/31/attention-authors-updated-practice-for-review-articles-and-position-papers-in-arxiv-cs-category/)
- **arXiv moderation**: "Submissions that do not contain original or substantive research ... may be declined". ## 5. How compatible is each high-impact venue's AI policy with the disclosed workflow, and how should it be presented?

### Takeaway
Every target venue permits substantial, disclosed AI assistance with human accountability: the Nature Portfolio and Springer Nature ("Amber"), PLOS, eLife, Science, PNAS and Elsevier/Cell Press. None permits AI authorship. The weak points are as follows:
- Elsevier/Cell Press frames acceptable use as "limited, supportive" and lists "Generating sections of a manuscript without genuine intellectual contribution" as inappropriate.
- PNAS screens submissions with the AI detector Pangram.
- SocArXiv, Issues in Science and Technology and Times Higher Education reject AI-drafted text.

Present the workflow as method: who decided what, what the agents did, how outputs were verified, and where the record is archived.

### Cited Findings
- **Nature Portfolio and Springer Nature** (risk framework):

### Inferences
- **Classification**: under the Springer Nature framework, the workflow is "Amber". The agents did literature search, citation checks and first drafting; the author set the thesis, structure, judgements and final revision. That is true here, and the §4b hypothesis is explicitly the author's.
- **Elsevier and Cell Press are the grey zone.** First drafting of whole sections exceeds "limited, supportive" on a plain reading. Patterns is therefore not recommended as a lead target.
- **Present the workflow as a method, in five elements:**
  1. a named section in the main text ("How this paper was made"), not only a footnote;
  2. the division of labour;
  3. the verification procedure;
  4. an archived process record with a DOI;
  5. the time and cost data.
  In the cover letter, cite the venue's own policy tier or wording, and offer the editors the full record. Use "AI-assisted drafting under the author's direction" rather than "AI-written".
- **Science**: the cover-letter disclosure is mandatory. **PNAS**: expect a Pangram flag; disclosure pre-empts it, but editors may still be wary.

## 7. What are the main risks?

### Takeaway
The main risks, in order:
1. **Crowding.** Nature-family editors have run several close pieces in 2026, and two journal articles (Manabu, Hansen) make near-identical claims.
2. **The irony critique.** One more paper, or three, in a system that counts papers.
3. **Disclosure-triggered rejection** at venues with restrictive or detector-based screening.
4. **Time lost to post-review rejection** at prestige venues. Desk rejections are fast.
5. **Credibility contamination** from MDPI, Frontiers or Hindawi-style special issues, or from AI-only outlets such as aiXiv.
6. **Media framing as a stunt**, which also carries a reputational risk for EMBL and UPF.

### Cited Findings
- **Crowding**:
  - Nature published Comments by Messeri & Crockett (19 May 2026) and Braun (1 Sep 2026), and Correspondences by Hutchinson (29 Sep) and Motoki (6 Oct) on AI and the production of science. — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02686-z); [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-01557-x)
  - Manabu 2026 (PSE) and Hansen 2026 (RIPR) are close to the thesis. — via [4_journal_venues.md §5](4_journal_venues.md)
- **Volume pressures**: AI conferences imposed submission caps (Nature news, 13 Oct 2026). — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03153-5)
- **Special issues**: the literature report records Hanson et al.'s finding that special issues helped drive article growth at some publishers. — [Hanson et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1162/qss_a_00327) (summary in `reports/AI disruption of academia literature.md`)
- **MDPI**: in 2023 Clarivate delisted two MDPI titles and 19 Hindawi titles. — via [4_journal_venues.md §8](4_journal_venues.md) (secondary sources)
- **aiXiv** "accepts work produced by large language models". — [SocArXiv AI policy](https://socopen.org/ai-policy/)
- **Stunt framing**: the 2022 GPT-3 "paper about itself" drew broad coverage. Searches found no confirmed journal outcome. — [Scientific American (search summary)](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/we-asked-gpt-3-to-write-an-academic-paper-about-itself-then-we-tried-to-get-it-published/)
- **Time cost of rejection after review**: median submission to acceptance is 252 days at NHB and 321 at Nature. Average peer review at PLOS Biology is 42 days. — [NHB metrics](https://www.nature.com/nathumbehav/journal-impact); [Nature metrics](https://www.nature.com/nature/journal-impact); [PLOS Biology](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/s/journal-information)

### Inferences
- **Crowding**: differentiate in the first paragraph and the cover letter. Accountability is not enough (contra Braun). Scarcity-based signals fail even under perfect accountability. The paper documents its own production.
- **Irony critique**:
  - Concede it in the text.
  - Cap the outputs at one peer-reviewed essay, one short piece and one preprint.
  - Never submit in parallel.
  - Publish the time and cost data.
  - Optionally state that the author will not use the piece as a counted output in formal evaluation. This is a gesture; whether it is wise for the author's career is their call.
- **Rejection triggered by disclosure**: avoid class (b) and (c) venues and Cell Press as lead targets, and put the disclosure where each policy asks for it.
- **Time**: a worst-case path (NHB rejected after review, then PLOS Biology, then EMBO Reports) runs about 6–9 months. A desk-rejection path costs about 3–5 weeks in total.
- **Outsider perception**: specialist reviewers will test command of the metascience literature. The AI-verified bibliography of about 150 works is an asset, but only if the condensed version keeps the key counter-evidence (Fanelli; Korytkowski & Kulczycki; Petersen; Kusumegi's productivity gains).

### Gaps
- No primary Clarivate or Frontiers source was retrieved.
- No evidence was found on how editors treat openly AI-drafted opinion pieces in practice. This is untested territory.
