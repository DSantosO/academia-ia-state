# Section 5 — Limitations and risks of relying on privately developed AI for science and knowledge

Thesis under examination: current frontier models are built by a few private companies that harvest humanity's knowledge (including through chat interactions), building a "global mind" controlled by a few firms.

Verification method: every DOI below was resolved against the Crossref API (title, authors, venue, volume/pages checked) on 2026-10-15; arXiv IDs checked against the arXiv API; report/book/web URLs checked to return HTTP 200 with the matching page title (MIT Press pages returned 403 to automated fetch, so those books are cited via DOI or a verified review instead). Labels: **[S]** supports, **[N]** nuances, **[C]** challenges the thesis.

## Q1. Concentration of AI, compute and research agenda-setting in a few firms

### Takeaway
Strong, peer-reviewed evidence that industry now dominates frontier model production, compute, talent and benchmarks, and that "open" branding does not undo this concentration. This is the empirical core supporting the thesis' "few firms" claim; economists frame it as structural (scale economies), not only as corporate strategy.

### Cited Findings
- **[S] Ahmed, N., Wahed, M., & Thompson, N. C. (2023). The growing influence of industry in AI research. *Science*, 379(6635), 884–886. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.ade2420** — Documents industry's growing dominance of AI inputs (compute, data, talent) and outputs (largest models, benchmark leadership), warning that public-interest research directions may be crowded out. — [Crossref/DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.ade2420)
- **[S] Whittaker, M. (2021). The steep cost of capture. *Interactions*, 28(6), 50–55. https://doi.org/10.1145/3488666** — Argues big tech's control of compute, data and funding has "captured" academic AI research, shaping which questions get asked and which critiques survive. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3488666)
- **[S] Widder, D. G., West, S. M., & Whittaker, M. (2023). Open (for business): Big tech, concentrated power, and the political economy of open AI. SSRN working paper. https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4543807**; published version: **Widder, D. G., Whittaker, M., & West, S. M. (2024). Why 'open' AI systems are actually closed, and why this matters. *Nature*, 635, 827–833. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08141-1** — Shows that "open" AI (open weights, open APIs) rarely removes dependence on the compute, data, labour and frameworks controlled by a few firms; openness can even serve incumbents. Note author order differs between versions. — [SSRN DOI](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4543807); [Nature DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08141-1)
- **[S] Stanford HAI (2025). *The 2025 AI Index Report*. https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report** — "Nearly 90% of notable AI models in 2024 came from industry, up from 60% in 2023"; training compute doubles about every five months. **[N]** The same report says academia "remains the top source of highly cited research" and open-weight models cut the gap with closed models "from 8% to just 1.7% on some benchmarks in a single year." — [AI Index 2025](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report)
- **[S] Brennan, K., Kak, A., & West, S. M. (2025). *Artificial Power: 2025 Landscape Report*. AI Now Institute, 3 June 2025. https://ainowinstitute.org/2025-landscape** — Argues that pushing AI products into everyday use gives AI firms power "that goes far beyond their deep pockets" and that AI is "being used on us"; sets out a policy roadmap. Mostly US-focused. — [AI Now](https://ainowinstitute.org/2025-landscape); [InfoDocket summary](https://www.infodocket.com/2025/06/03/ai-now-institute-releases-artificial-power-2025-landscape-report/)
- **[S/N] Besiroglu, T., Bergerson, S. A., Michael, A., et al. (2024). The compute divide in machine learning: A threat to academic contribution and scrutiny? arXiv:2401.02452. https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02452** — Quantifies how academia's share of large-compute models has fallen and argues this limits academics' ability to scrutinise frontier systems. Preprint (not checked for a peer-reviewed version). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02452)
- **[N] Vipra, J., & Korinek, A. (2023). Market concentration implications of foundation models. arXiv:2311.01550 (Brookings working paper). https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01550**; follow-up **Korinek, A., & Vipra, J. (2025). Concentrating intelligence: Scaling and market structure in artificial intelligence. *Economic Policy*, 40(121), 225–256** (NBER WP 33139, https://doi.org/10.3386/w33139) — Economic analysis of why fixed costs and scale economies push frontier models toward natural-monopoly-like structure, plus a discussion of balancing competition and safety. This supports "a few firms" but explains it as structural economics rather than deliberate harvesting. Journal volume and pages come from RePEc as reported in search results; the NBER DOI was resolved directly. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01550); [NBER DOI](https://doi.org/10.3386/w33139); [Korinek RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/e/pko142.html)

### Inferences
- "Few firms" is well supported for frontier training and compute. It is less clear-cut for research output: academia still leads in highly cited papers, and the open–closed performance gap is shrinking. The paper should say "frontier capability and infrastructure are concentrated" rather than "all AI is concentrated".

### Gaps
- I did not obtain the full text of AI Index 2025 chapter tables (e.g. exact industry share of AI PhD hires). Ahmed et al. 2023 is the peer-reviewed source to use for talent-flow statistics.
- I did not locate a separate AI Now 2023 "Confronting Tech Power" report URL. The 2025 report is the current flagship.

## Q2. Data extraction and enclosure of the knowledge commons (scholarly literature, chat logs, Q&A sites)

### Takeaway
There is solid documentary evidence that (a) scholarly publishers sold access to research for LLM training without author opt-out, (b) at least one major provider changed consumer defaults to train on chat transcripts unless users opt out, and (c) public knowledge commons (Stack Overflow) shrank after ChatGPT while web data is increasingly fenced off. Theoretical framings (surveillance capitalism, data colonialism, knowledge commons) give the thesis its conceptual vocabulary.

### Cited Findings
- **[S] Gibney, E. (2024). Has your paper been used to train an AI model? Almost certainly. *Nature*, 632, 715–716. https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-024-02599-9** — Nature news feature on publishers licensing research content to AI developers, and the likelihood that most openly available papers are already in training sets. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-024-02599-9)
- **[S] Taylor & Francis–Microsoft deal (2024).** Taylor & Francis (Informa) licensed research content to Microsoft for AI training, worth about £8m/$10m in the first year. Academics said they were not told and had no opt-out; Informa reported over $75m in AI partnership revenue in 2024. — [ISSN summary of The Bookseller report](https://www.issn.org/newsletter_issn/academic-authors-shocked-after-taylor-francis-sells-access-to-their-research-to-microsoft-ai/); [Bloomberg Law, June 2025](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/ip-law/academic-publishers-sign-ai-deals-as-trump-cuts-research-funding)
- **[S] Wiley AI licensing (2024).** Wiley was set to earn $44m from two AI content-licensing deals with unnamed tech firms ($23m already earned) and confirmed "no specific opt-out for authors." — [The Bookseller](https://thebookseller.com/news/wiley-set-to-earn-44m-from-ai-rights-deals-confirms-no-opt-out-for-authors)
- **[S] Anthropic (2025). Updates to Consumer Terms and Privacy Policy. https://www.anthropic.com/news/updates-to-our-consumer-terms** — Consumer (Free/Pro/Max, including Claude Code) chats and coding sessions may be used for model training unless the user opts out. Retention is extended to five years for users who allow training, versus 30 days for those who opt out. Business, education, government and API use are excluded. Critics noted the setting is on by default. A clear primary-source example of "harvesting through chat interactions". Secondary reports give different decision deadlines (28 Sept vs 8 Oct 2025). — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/updates-to-our-consumer-terms); [Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/amp/technology/news/story/anthropic-to-use-claude-conversations-for-ai-training-unless-users-opt-out-by-september-28-491530-2025-08-29)
- **[S] del Rio-Chanona, R. M., Laurentsyeva, N., & Wachs, J. (2024). Large language models reduce public knowledge sharing on online Q&A platforms. *PNAS Nexus*, 3(9), pgae400. https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae400** — Finds that activity on Stack Overflow fell after ChatGPT's release compared with counterfactual platforms. Knowledge exchange moves into private chat interfaces, which depletes the public commons that future models (and humans) rely on. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae400)
- **[N] Longpre, S., Mahari, R., Lee, A., Lund, C., et al. (2024). Consent in crisis: The rapid decline of the AI data commons. *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 37*, 108042–108087. https://doi.org/10.52202/079017-3431** — Audits web domains and shows rapidly rising robots.txt and terms-of-service restrictions on AI crawling. Creators are pushing back against extraction, but the restrictions may also entrench incumbents that already scraped data or can pay for licences. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.52202/079017-3431)
- **[S] Zuboff, S. (2019). *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism: The Fight for a Human Future at the New Frontier of Power*. New York: PublicAffairs. ISBN 9781610395694.** — Classic theory of "behavioural surplus" extraction from user interactions, directly applicable to chat-log harvesting. — [Hachette](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/shoshana-zuboff/the-age-of-surveillance-capitalism/9781610395694/)
- **[S] Couldry, N., & Mejias, U. A. (2019). *The Costs of Connection: How Data Is Colonizing Human Life and Appropriating It for Capitalism*. Stanford University Press. https://doi.org/10.1515/9781503609754**; and **Couldry, N., & Mejias, U. A. (2019). Data colonialism: Rethinking big data's relation to the contemporary subject. *Television & New Media*, 20(4), 336–349. https://doi.org/10.1177/1527476418796632** — Frames data appropriation as a new colonial-style extraction of social life. — [Book DOI](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781503609754); [Article DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1527476418796632)
- **[N] Hess, C., & Ostrom, E. (Eds.) (2007). *Understanding Knowledge as a Commons: From Theory to Practice*. MIT Press. https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/6980.001.0001** — Foundational account of scholarly knowledge as a commons, with its own governance problems such as enclosure and free-riding. It gives the vocabulary for "enclosure" and also points to commons-based governance as a remedy. Crossref dates the online edition to Dec 2006; the print edition is 2007. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/6980.001.0001)

### Inferences
- The "harvesting via chats" claim is best supported with primary provider policies (Anthropic 2025 is verified here) plus del Rio-Chanona et al. on displacement from public Q&A. The publisher deals show that extraction of scholarly knowledge also happens through *licensing by intermediaries*, not only scraping. The paper's framing should therefore include publishers as co-beneficiaries.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch OpenAI or Google consumer data-use policies in this pass. The paper should cite their current help-centre pages directly if it names them.
- Wiley FY2026 AI-revenue figures (reported by CASRAI, July 2026) were not verified against Wiley filings, so I excluded them.

## Q3. Epistemic risks for science (illusions of understanding, monoculture, collapse, narrowing, reproducibility)

### Takeaway
A growing body of high-profile work, much of it empirical, shows that reliance on a small set of AI systems can narrow scientific attention and homogenise outputs, create illusions of understanding, degrade future models trained on AI output, and undermine reproducibility when the models are proprietary. Together these supply the strongest "why it matters for science" arguments.

### Cited Findings
- **[S] Messeri, L., & Crockett, M. J. (2024). Artificial intelligence and illusions of understanding in scientific research. *Nature*, 627, 49–58. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07146-0** — Maps AI "visions" in science (oracle, surrogate, quant, arbiter) and argues that they risk illusions of explanatory depth, breadth and objectivity, and "monocultures of knowing". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07146-0)
- **[S] Hao, Q., Xu, F., Li, Y., & Evans, J. (2026). Artificial intelligence tools expand scientists' impact but contract science's focus. *Nature*, 649, 1237–1243. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09922-y** — Large-scale bibliometric study: AI-using scientists publish more and get more citations, but AI-enabled research clusters into a narrower set of topics, so science collectively contracts its focus. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09922-y)
- **[S] Doshi, A. R., & Hauser, O. P. (2024). Generative AI enhances individual creativity but reduces the collective diversity of novel content. *Science Advances*, 10(28), eadn5290. https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290** — Experiment showing that GenAI-assisted stories were rated more creative individually but were more similar to each other. This is the individual-versus-collective trade-off behind homogenisation. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290)
- **[S] Kleinberg, J., & Raghavan, M. (2021). Algorithmic monoculture and social welfare. *PNAS*, 118(22), e2018340118. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2018340118** — Formal model showing that when many decision-makers use the same algorithm, overall accuracy can fall even if that algorithm is individually better. This is the theoretical basis for monoculture risk. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2018340118)
- **[S] Bommasani, R., Creel, K. A., Kumar, A., Jurafsky, D., & Liang, P. (2022). Picking on the same person: Does algorithmic monoculture lead to outcome homogenization? *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 35*, 3663–3678. https://doi.org/10.52202/068431-0265** — Shows that sharing training data or foundation models across deployments makes the same individuals fail systematically. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.52202/068431-0265)
- **[S] Shumailov, I., Shumaylov, Z., Zhao, Y., Papernot, N., Anderson, R., & Gal, Y. (2024). AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data. *Nature*, 631, 755–759. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07566-y** — Training on model-generated data causes the tails of the distribution to disappear ("model collapse"). This underlines the value of human-generated commons, the same commons that Q2 shows are eroding. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07566-y)
- **[S] Bender, E. M., Gebru, T., McMillan-Major, A., & Shmitchell, S. (2021). On the dangers of stochastic parrots: Can language models be too big? *Proceedings of FAccT '21*, 610–623. https://doi.org/10.1145/3442188.3445922** — Early critique covering the environmental cost, undocumented and hegemonic training data, and fluent output without meaning. It also shows how scale favours a few resource-rich actors. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3442188.3445922)
- **[S] Palmer, A., Smith, N. A., & Spirling, A. (2024). Using proprietary language models in academic research requires explicit justification. *Nature Computational Science*, 4, 2–3. https://doi.org/10.1038/s43588-023-00585-1** — Argues that closed, silently updated models threaten reproducibility, transparency and research ethics. Researchers should justify using them over open alternatives. Crossref lists online publication on 21 Dec 2023. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43588-023-00585-1)
- **[S] Spirling, A. (2023). Why open-source generative AI models are an ethical way forward for science. *Nature*, 616, 413. https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-023-01295-4** — World View piece: dependence on proprietary LLMs undermines reproducibility and scientific independence, so scientists should back open models. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-023-01295-4)

### Inferences
- Hao et al. (2026) and Doshi & Hauser (2024) provide *empirical* support for the "global mind" worry in its strongest form, homogenisation of collective thought. Kleinberg & Raghavan and Bommasani et al. explain why this follows from shared models whoever owns them. **[N]** Monoculture risk therefore arises from shared models even when they are open. Private ownership adds opacity and control, but it is not the only cause.

### Gaps
- I found no direct empirical study linking *chat-log harvesting specifically* to scientific homogenisation. That link is an inference.

## Q4. "Global brain" / "world brain" and LLMs as aggregated collective knowledge

### Takeaway
The "global mind" metaphor has a long lineage, from Wells' World Brain to Heylighen's Global Brain. The most authoritative recent framing (Farrell et al., Science 2025) treats LLMs as *cultural and social technologies*, comparable to markets, bureaucracies and libraries, that aggregate human knowledge, rather than as minds. This both supports and disciplines the thesis' metaphor.

### Cited Findings
- **[N] Farrell, H., Gopnik, A., Shalizi, C., & Evans, J. (2025). Large AI models are cultural and social technologies. *Science*, 387(6739), 1153–1156. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adt9819** — Argues that large models are best understood as new ways of summarising and transmitting accumulated human knowledge, like writing, print, markets and bureaucracies, not as agents or minds. This supports "aggregated humanity's knowledge" but argues against anthropomorphic "global mind" language. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adt9819)
- **[N] Wells, H. G. (1938/2021). *World Brain*. Foreword by Bruce Sterling, introduction by Joseph Reagle. MIT Press, 2021.** — Wells' 1930s proposal for a permanent world encyclopaedia as a shared, *publicly* organised "world brain". Useful as a contrast: the original vision was public and open, unlike today's privately owned aggregations. The 2021 edition is verified via a Science review: **Benkler, Y. (2021). The alternative to despair is to build an ark. *Science*. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abk0210** — [Benkler review DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abk0210)
- **[N] Heylighen, F., & Lenartowicz, M. (2017). The Global Brain as a model of the future information society: An introduction to the special issue. *Technological Forecasting and Social Change*, 114, 1–6. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.techfore.2016.10.063** — Introduces the "Global Brain" concept: the networked human–ICT system as a distributed, self-organising intelligence. In Heylighen's version it is decentralised, which contrasts with the thesis' centralised, firm-controlled "global mind". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.techfore.2016.10.063)

### Inferences
- The paper can use Wells and Heylighen to argue that the global-brain ideal was public and distributed, and that what has emerged is a *privatised, centralised* version. Farrell et al. supply the scholarly basis for calling LLMs aggregations of human cultural knowledge.

### Gaps
- I did not verify a peer-reviewed source that explicitly uses "global mind controlled by a few firms". The phrase appears to be the author's own framing and should be presented as such.

## Q5. Alternatives: open models and public/sovereign AI

### Takeaway
Credible, fully open alternatives exist at substantial scale: BLOOM, OLMo and Apertus release weights and data, and Apertus is a national public effort. A coherent "public AI" policy agenda has also emerged. Widder et al. caution, however, that openness alone does not dissolve concentration in compute and infrastructure.

### Cited Findings
- **[N] BigScience Workshop (Le Scao, T., et al.) (2022). BLOOM: A 176B-parameter open-access multilingual language model. arXiv:2211.05100. https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05100** — A model built by a large international research collaboration with open governance. It is the proof of concept for non-corporate, collaborative LLM development (trained on the public Jean Zay supercomputer). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05100)
- **[N] Groeneveld, D., Beltagy, I., Walsh, P., Bhagia, A., Kinney, R., et al. (2024). OLMo: Accelerating the science of language models. *Proceedings of ACL 2024 (Vol. 1)*, 15789–15809. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2024.acl-long.841** — A fully open LLM from AI2, releasing weights, training data, code and logs, explicitly so that scientists can study language models. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2024.acl-long.841)
- **[N] Project Apertus (Hernández-Cano, A., Hägele, A., et al.) (2025). Apertus: Democratizing open and compliant LLMs for global language environments. arXiv:2509.14233. https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14233** — A Swiss public model from EPFL, ETH Zurich and CSCS, trained on the public Alps supercomputer. It comes in 8B and 70B sizes, was trained on about 15T tokens in 1,000+ languages (about 40% non-English), and releases data, weights and checkpoints. It was launched 2 September 2025. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14233); [USI news](https://www.usi.ch/en/feeds/33046)
- **[N] Public AI Network (2024). *Public AI: Infrastructure for the Common Good* (white paper, Aug 2024, rev. Oct 2024; CC BY 4.0). https://publicai.network/whitepaper** — Argues for AI provisioned as public infrastructure, like libraries or utilities, with public access, public accountability and permanent public goods. A related but distinct document is the Bertelsmann Stiftung et al. (2025) "Public AI White Paper – A public alternative to private AI dominance" ([PDF](https://www.bertelsmann-stiftung.de/fileadmin/files/BSt/Bibliothek/Doi_Publikationen/Public_AI_2025_01.pdf)); do not conflate the two. — [Public AI Network](https://publicai.network/whitepaper); [Berkman Klein summary](https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2024-08/public-ai)

### Inferences
- Apertus and BLOOM show that public supercomputing can produce competitive open models. The constraint is less technical capability than sustained public funding and access to data.

### Gaps
- No verified primary source was gathered here for "CERN for AI" proposals (e.g. CAIRNE/CLAIRE) or EU AI Factories. These should be fetched separately if cited.
- Not verified: EleutherAI's GPT-NeoX/Pythia papers (well known, but DOIs not resolved in this pass).

## Q6. Counter-arguments (open weights, competition, benefits of industry investment)

### Takeaway
The main counter-evidence is that open-weight models have nearly closed the benchmark gap, inference costs have collapsed, and analysts argue the marginal risks of open models are often overstated while their benefits for competition and scrutiny are real. Industry investment has also produced tools that, by Hao et al.'s own measure, boost individual scientists' impact. These points nuance the thesis without overturning the evidence of concentration at the frontier.

### Cited Findings
- **[C] Kapoor, S., Bommasani, R., Klyman, K., et al. (2024). Position: On the societal impact of open foundation models. *Proceedings of ICML 2024*, PMLR 235. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/kapoor24a.html** (arXiv:2403.07918) — Sets out the benefits of open foundation models (distributing power, enabling innovation, enabling scientific research and transparency) and a framework showing their marginal risk is often small. Open weights are thus a partial counterweight to concentration. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/kapoor24a.html)
- **[C] Stanford HAI (2025). AI Index.** The open–closed gap narrowed from 8% to 1.7% on some benchmarks in one year, and GPT-3.5-level inference cost fell more than 280-fold between Nov 2022 and Oct 2024. This suggests rapid diffusion and lower barriers to *use*, if not to frontier *training*. — [AI Index 2025](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report)
- **[C/N] Hao et al. (2026, *Nature*)** — AI tools raise individual scientists' productivity and citation impact, a real benefit even as collective focus narrows. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09922-y)
- **[N] Widder, Whittaker & West (2024, *Nature*)** — The rebuttal to the counter-argument: open weights still depend on concentrated compute, data and ecosystems, and some of the leading open-weight models are released by the same large firms. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08141-1)
- **[N] Korinek & Vipra (2023/2025)** — Concentration may be partly an efficient result of scale economies, so remedies should focus on competition policy and access rather than assume bad faith. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01550)

### Inferences
- The most defensible version of the thesis is: *the frontier of capability, the infrastructure, and the flow of new interaction data are concentrated in a few firms, and this creates epistemic and governance risks for science.* Sweeping claims that all AI knowledge is controlled by a few firms are contradicted by open-weight diffusion and public models.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous empirical study measuring competitive pressure from open-weight models on frontier-lab pricing or behaviour beyond AI Index aggregate trends.
- I did not retrieve industry-funded studies of the benefits of private R&D investment for science (e.g. AlphaFold's impact). The paper could cite AlphaFold-related literature as a counterexample of industry-produced public goods, but it was not verified here.
