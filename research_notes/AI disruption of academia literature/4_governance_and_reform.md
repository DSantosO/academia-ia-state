## Q1. Reform initiatives for research assessment, and evidence on their uptake and effect

### Takeaway
There is a dense, decade-long stack of reform declarations (DORA 2012 → Leiden 2015 → Metric Tide 2015 → Hong Kong 2020 → UNESCO 2021 → CoARA 2022). Uptake looks good on paper: hundreds of signatories, a growing number of national agencies including Spain's ANECA. But the evidence on effects is mostly self-reported action plans, and the European Commission's own 2026 baseline study calls progress "uneven". The reforms target *how researchers are counted*, not *what science is for*. None was designed with AI-driven output inflation in mind.

### Cited Findings (annotated bibliography)
1. **San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment (DORA)** (2012, published 2013). https://sfdora.org/read/ [URL-blocked; universally cited]. This is the founding declaration: it calls for journal-based metrics such as the JIF not to be used as a surrogate for the quality of individual articles or researchers in hiring, promotion and funding. **NUANCES**: it shows the problem has been recognised for more than a decade, and that recognition has not translated into a changed system.
2. **Hicks, D., Wouters, P., Waltman, L., de Rijcke, S. & Rafols, I. (2015). Bibliometrics: The Leiden Manifesto for research metrics. *Nature* 520, 429–431.** https://doi.org/10.1038/520429a [DOI✓]. This paper sets out 10 principles: quantitative evaluation should support qualitative expert assessment, measure performance against the mission, and so on. **NUANCES**: it is the canonical "responsible metrics" statement, and it is voluntary.
3. **Wilsdon, J. et al. (2015). *The Metric Tide: Independent Review of the Role of Metrics in Research Assessment and Management*. HEFCE/SAGE.** https://doi.org/10.4135/9781473978782 [DOI✓]. This UK review concluded that metrics should support, not supplant, peer review, and it coined "responsible metrics". **NUANCES.**
4. **Curry, S., Gadd, E. & Wilsdon, J. (2022). *Harnessing the Metric Tide: indicators, infrastructures & priorities for UK responsible research assessment*. Research on Research Institute.** https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.21701624 [DOI✓ via DataCite]. This seven-year follow-up found real but partial progress, with recommendations for REF 2028 and for research culture. **NUANCES**: it documents that the change was slow, even in the country that started the movement.
5. **Moher, D., Bouter, L., Kleinert, S., Glasziou, P., Sham, M.H., Barbour, V., Coriat, A.-M., Foeger, N. & Dirnagl, U. (2020). The Hong Kong Principles for assessing researchers: Fostering research integrity. *PLOS Biology* 18(7): e3000737.** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3000737 [DOI✓]. 6. **Moher, D. et al. (2018). Assessing scientists for hiring, promotion, and tenure. *PLOS Biology* 16(3): e2004089.** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.2004089 [DOI✓]. This review of criteria actually in use found that the dominant ones are publication counts, impact factors and grant income, while practices that reward rigour are rarely assessed. **SUPPORTS**: it is the empirical baseline that incentives were not aligned with good science.

### Inferences
- Reform instruments are almost all **voluntary, declarative and signatory-based**. Evidence of effect is mostly counts of signatories and action plans. Outcome evaluations, such as whether hiring decisions actually changed, are rare.
- None of the major instruments (DORA, Leiden, Hong Kong, CoARA) was written with generative AI in view. They target metric misuse, not machine-scale output. Spain's ANECA AI-use statement is an early and rare exception.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous before/after study of DORA signatories' hiring outcomes. One may exist; it was not located in this pass.
- I could not access the full text of the EC 2026 ARRA baseline study. The 263/235/15 figures come from a secondary summary only.
- The CASRAI claim that about 78% of CoARA action-plan entries are "Pending" was **not verified** and should not be used.

## Q2. Why reform is slow: collective action, rankings, path dependence, critiques of CoARA/DORA

### Takeaway
The literature points to a coordination trap. Individual institutions that unilaterally drop metrics risk losing standing in global rankings and talent markets, and rankings themselves manufacture competition. Selection pressures favour high-output labs. Reform has also become a "professional movement" with internal critics on both flanks: scientometricians who see "bibliometric denialism", and philosophers who see EC-driven administrative capture.

### Cited Findings (annotated bibliography)
18. **Smaldino, P.E. & McElreath, R. (2016). The natural selection of bad science. *Royal Society Open Science* 3: 160384.** https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.160384 [DOI✓]. An evolutionary model shows that incentives rewarding publication quantity select for low-rigour methods, even without individual misconduct. Replication alone cannot fix it; institutional incentive change is needed. **SUPPORTS**: this is the core mechanism argument.
19. **Brembs, B., Button, K. & Munafò, M. (2013). Deep impact: unintended consequences of journal rank. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 7: 291.** https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2013.00291 [DOI resolves via doi.org✓]. Journal rank predicts reliability poorly or even negatively, yet structures careers. **SUPPORTS.**
20. **Brankovic, J., Ringel, L. & Werron, T. (2018). How rankings produce competition: the case of global university rankings. *Zeitschrift für Soziologie* 47(4).** https://doi.org/10.1515/zfsoz-2018-0118 [DOI✓]. This sociological, institutional-theory account shows that rankings do not merely measure competition among universities but *create* it. **SUPPORTS**: rankings are a structural obstacle to unilateral reform.
21. **Hazelkorn, E. (2015). *Rankings and the Reshaping of Higher Education: The Battle for World-Class Excellence* (2nd ed.). Palgrave Macmillan.** https://doi.org/10.1057/9781137446671 (chapter DOIs e.g. 10.1057/9781137446671_6 [DOI✓]). It documents how THE, QS and ARWU shape institutional strategy and national policy. **SUPPORTS.**
22. **Gadd, E. (2020). University rankings need a rethink. *Nature* 587, 523.** https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-020-03312-2 [DOI✓]. A more systematic companion is **Gadd, E., Holmes, R. & Shearer, J. (2021). Developing a method for evaluating global university rankings. *Scholarly Assessment Reports* 3(1).** https://doi.org/10.29024/sar.31 [DOI✓]. Rankings fail on rigour, transparency and fairness criteria, yet universities keep feeding them. **SUPPORTS.**
23. **Hatch, A. & Curry, S. (2020). Changing how we evaluate research is difficult, but not impossible. *eLife* 9: e58654.** https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.58654 [DOI✓]. The DORA leadership names barriers: entrenched habits, the convenience of metrics, and the need for coordinated multi-actor change. It offers practical tools. **NUANCES**: it is the reformers' own diagnosis of slowness.
24. **Rushforth, A. & Hammarfelt, B. (2023). The rise of responsible metrics as a professional reform movement: a collective action frames account. *Quantitative Science Studies* 4(4), 879–897.** https://doi.org/10.1162/qss_a_00280 [DOI✓]. It analyses "responsible metrics" as a movement led by scientometric and administrative professionals, with framing that shapes what reforms are imaginable. **NUANCES.**
25. **Baccini, A. (2025). COARA will not save science from the tyranny of administrative evaluation. *Research Evaluation*.** https://doi.org/10.1093/reseval/rvaf024 (preprint arXiv:2408.05587, verified) [DOI from arXiv metadata]. Drawing on Kitcher's "well-ordered science", it argues CoARA keeps evaluation administrative and expert-captured: governments and agencies define quality and impact. It argues for *limiting* administrative evaluation. **SUPPORTS**: governance is reconfiguring control rather than rethinking purpose.
26. **Abramo, G. (2024). The forced battle between peer-review and scientometric research assessment: why the CoARA initiative is unsound. *Research Evaluation*.** https://doi.org/10.1093/reseval/rvae021 [DOI✓]. It argues CoARA's privileging of peer review over well-normalised metrics is unsound, especially for STEMM, and is costly and biased. **CHALLENGES** the reform movement from the other side, as a critique of reform, not of the status quo.
27. **Torres-Salinas, D., Arroyo-Machado, W. & Robinson-Garcia, N. (2023). Bibliometric denialism. *Scientometrics* 128, 5357–5359.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-023-04787-2 [DOI✓]. This short communication by Spanish scientometricians warns that anti-metric rhetoric in reform risks discarding useful evidence. **CHALLENGES** the reform movement. It is relevant to the Spanish debate.
28. **Mirowski, P. (2018). The future(s) of open science. *Social Studies of Science* 48(2), 171–203.** https://doi.org/10.1177/0306312718772086 [DOI✓]. A critical STS reading argues that "open science" platforms risk re-engineering science for commercial and platform logics rather than fixing its pathologies. **SUPPORTS/NUANCES**: reform discourse can be captured.

### Inferences
- Slowness is over-determined. Global rankings and international mobility markets punish unilateral exit (collective-action trap), and evaluation professionals shape reform from within (Rushforth & Hammarfelt). The reform coalition is itself split between metric-sceptics and metric-defenders. In Spain, this split is visible between ANECA's CoARA-aligned reform and Granada-based scientometric critics.
- AI intensifies the trap. If AI lowers the marginal cost of papers, any metric still counting outputs inflates. Institutions that keep counting gain in rankings in the short term. This is an inference, not an empirically demonstrated finding.

### Gaps
- I found no empirical study quantifying how much rankings position constrains CoARA signatories' actual practices.
- No source found applies institutional theory (isomorphism, decoupling) directly to CoARA implementation. "Decoupling" between signed commitments and practice is a plausible framing, but I did not find it tested.

## Q3. Slow science, metascience, and new research organisations and funding models

### Takeaway
There is a rich alternative imaginary, including slow science, metascience, focused research organisations (FROs), lotteries, ARIA and Arc. But most of it sits *outside* or *alongside* traditional universities and funders, which supports the thesis that mainstream governance is not where the reinvention is happening.

### Cited Findings (annotated bibliography)
29. **Stengers, I. (2018). *Another Science is Possible: A Manifesto for Slow Science*. Polity. ISBN 978-1-5095-2180-7 (hb) / 978-1-5095-2181-4 (pb).** [search✓; retailer listings and the book's front matter]. A philosophical critique of "fast science", the knowledge economy and competitive excellence. It argues for reconnecting science with its publics. **SUPPORTS.**
30. **Frith, U. (2020). Fast lane to slow science. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 24(1), 1–2.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2019.10.007 [DOI✓]. It argues that the pressure to publish fast harms quality and well-being, and proposes practical steps toward slow science, such as limiting publication counts considered in evaluation. **SUPPORTS.**
31. **Ioannidis, J.P.A. (2005). Why most published research findings are false. *PLoS Medicine* 2(8): e124.** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.0020124 [DOI✓]. This foundational metascience paper is pre-2010 but canonical. **SUPPORTS** that the pathologies predate AI.
32. **Munafò, M.R. et al. (2017). A manifesto for reproducible science. *Nature Human Behaviour* 1: 0021.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-016-0021 [DOI✓]. It proposes measures across methods, reporting, reproducibility, evaluation and incentives, and notes that incentive change is the hardest. **SUPPORTS/NUANCES.**
33. **Nielsen, M. & Qiu, K. It argues science needs systematic institutional experimentation, with funders and institutions running and evaluating trials of new social processes, and that current institutions experiment far too little. **SUPPORTS.**
34. **Marblestone, A., Gamick, A., Kalil, T., Martin, C., Cvitkovic, M. & Rodriques, S.G. (2022). Unblock research bottlenecks with non-profit start-ups. *Nature* 601, 188–190.** https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-022-00018-5 [DOI✓]. This proposes focused research organisations (FROs) for mid-scale, team-based tool-building that fits neither academia nor industry. It is implemented through Convergent Research (https://www.convergentresearch.org/ [URL✓]). **SUPPORTS**: the reform happens by building new institutions outside universities.
35. **Fang, F.C. & Casadevall, A. (2016). Research funding: the case for a modified lottery. *mBio* 7(2): e00422-16.** https://doi.org/10.1128/mBio.00422-16 [DOI✓]. 37. **ARIA, the UK Advanced Research and Invention Agency** (est. 2023, ARPA-style, programme-director-led). https://www.aria.org.uk/ [URL✓]. **Arc Institute** (Palo Alto; funds investigators for renewable 8-year terms, independent of grants). https://arcinstitute.org/ [URL✓]. **NUANCES**: both are new-organisation experiments, created *outside* existing research councils and universities. ### Inferences
- The most radical experiments (FROs, Arc, ARIA, lotteries) were created by *bypassing* incumbent institutions, or as small pilots within funders. This supports the claim that incumbent governance is not itself moving toward "better, not more" science. It also challenges any claim that no alternatives exist.

### Gaps
- There is little outcome evidence yet for FROs, ARIA or Arc. They are too young.
- I found no Spanish or EU-level FRO/ARIA-equivalent in this pass. The proposed European "ARPA-like" instruments, such as the EIC Pathfinder, were not researched here.

## Q4. Governance responses to AI in research: are they reactive or inadequate?

### Takeaway
Governance responses since 2023 consist of journal and publisher authorship rules, living guidelines and non-binding EC guidance. They are fast but narrow: they regulate *disclosure and authorship*, not *what AI does to incentives and evaluation*. Evidence shows heterogeneous publisher policies and lagging university research policies.

### Cited Findings (annotated bibliography)
38. **Thorp, H.H. (2023). ChatGPT is fun, but not an author. *Science* 379(6630), 313.** https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adg7879 [DOI✓]. **Nature editorial (2023). Tools such as ChatGPT threaten transparent science; here are our ground rules for their use. *Nature* 613, 612.** https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-023-00191-1 [DOI✓]. These are the first reactive policies from leading journals: no LLM authorship, and disclosure of use. **SUPPORTS**: the response was reactive and focused on authorship.
39. **Bockting, C.L., van Dis, E.A.M., van Rooij, R., Zuidema, W. & Bollen, J. (2023). Living guidelines for generative AI — why scientists must oversee its use. *Nature* 622, 693–696.** https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-023-03266-1 [DOI✓]. It proposes an independent, scientist-led auditing body and "living" guidelines, because current regulation lags behind. **SUPPORTS**: by its authors' own premise, governance is not keeping up.
40. **European Commission / ERA Forum (2024). *Living guidelines on the responsible use of generative AI in research*. Published 20 March 2024.** News page: https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/news/all-research-and-innovation-news/guidelines-responsible-use-generative-ai-research-developed-european-research-area-forum-2024-03-20_en [URL✓]. ## Q5. Proposals to move beyond counting papers, and counter-arguments

### Takeaway
Alternatives to "the paper" and to counting are well articulated: narrative CVs, contribution-based evaluation, executable or computational papers, and modular publishing. But the evidence base for the main institutional alternative, narrative CVs, is thin and partly negative on bias. Critics from both scientometrics and the defence of traditional metrics argue reforms add noise, cost and new biases.

### Cited Findings (annotated bibliography)
48. **Somers, J. (2018, April 5). The scientific paper is obsolete: here's what's next. *The Atlantic*.** https://www.theatlantic.com/science/archive/2018/04/the-scientific-paper-is-obsolete/556676/ [URL-blocked; search✓ via multiple independent citations]. It argues the PDF paper cannot carry code-dependent science, and points to Mathematica and Jupyter notebooks as alternatives. **SUPPORTS**: the unit of output is itself outdated.
49. **Nielsen, M. (2011). *Reinventing Discovery: The New Era of Networked Science*. Princeton University Press.** https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400839452 [DOI✓]. It envisions networked, open, collaborative science beyond journal papers, and notes that incentives keep scientists from contributing to non-paper outputs. **SUPPORTS.**

### Inferences
- The counter-arguments mostly do *not* claim institutions are adapting effectively to AI. They claim the *current reform package* is poorly evidenced, or that it swaps one bias for another. I found **no source arguing that research governance is already adequately adapting to AI**. The closest is the EC 2026 baseline study's "broad and increasing engagement" in assessment reform, which concerns reform and not AI. That absence is itself a signal, but should be stated cautiously.
- For the position paper, the strongest honest framing is that reform machinery exists and is spreading, but it is voluntary and uneven. It is evidence-poor where it matters most (narrative CVs) and was designed before AI. The genuinely new models (eLife, Registered Reports, FROs, lotteries, Arc and ARIA) remain niche or sit outside incumbent institutions.
