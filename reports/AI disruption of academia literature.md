# AI accelerates, rather than causes, academia's crisis

The published literature strongly supports the position paper's core claim that AI is destabilising the academic system, but it supports a sharper version than "AI is destroying it." Three findings carry that version. First, the system was measurably strained before ChatGPT: indexed articles grew **about 47% between 2016 and 2022**, faster than the scientific workforce ([Hanson et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1162/qss_a_00327)). Second, peer review rested on **more than 100 million unpaid reviewer-hours a year** ([Aczel et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41073-021-00118-2)). Third, five national cases show researchers re-optimising precisely to whatever is counted. Generative AI then cut the cost of producing exactly those counted outputs. **At least 13.5% of 2024 biomedical abstracts** show LLM processing ([Kobak et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adt3813)), **about 21% of ICLR 2026 reviews** were flagged as fully AI-written ([Naddaf 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-025-03506-6)), and NIH capped applications at six per investigator after seeing PIs submit **more than 40 in one round** ([NIH 2025](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-25-132.html)). On section 3, the best evidence is double-edged: AI tools raise individual output and citations but **contract the collective range of topics by 4.63%** ([Hao et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09922-y)). On section 4, no source argues that governance is adapting adequately. On section 5, concentration of frontier capability in industry is well documented (**about 90% of notable 2024 models came from industry**, per [Stanford HAI 2025](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report)), but "global mind" is the author's own framing and has no direct scholarly anchor. Below are about 150 works grouped by the paper's five sections, followed by a register of flagged items and a list of the places where the thesis should be reframed.

## Section 1: metrics were bending behaviour long before ChatGPT

Across Australia, Spain, Poland, Italy and China, the literature documents researchers responding quickly and precisely to whatever evaluation counts. Australia's count-based funding formula raised volume but lowered relative impact. Italy's citation thresholds raised self-citation. China's cash rewards reached **USD 165,000 per paper** ([Quan et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1108/AJIM-01-2017-0014)). This is the empirical "fragile baseline" the paper needs. The counter-literature is narrower but real. Fanelli finds that diffuse publish-or-perish pressure does not predict retractions, though cash-per-paper does ([Fanelli et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0127556)). Polish data show that stable incentives raised output without a measured drop in quality ([Korytkowski & Kulczycki 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-019-03092-1)). The defensible claim is therefore "demonstrably gameable where incentives are crude," not "science was broken."

## Section 2: AI collapses the cost of what the system counts

Independent methods agree that LLM-modified text went from near zero to a large minority of scientific prose in about two years. The methods include excess vocabulary, distributional maximum-likelihood estimation, marker words and supervised detectors. Estimates reach **about 22% of computer-science abstract sentences** ([Liang et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02273-8)). The most consequential finding concerns evaluation itself: AI-assisted reviews at ICLR 2024 **raised scores and the acceptance odds of borderline papers** ([Latona et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.02150)). Hidden prompt injections now target AI reviewers ([Gibney 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-025-02172-y)), which creates an adversarial loop in which neither side is a human evaluator.

Two cautions belong in the paper. First, prevalence of AI *text* is not prevalence of AI-*fabricated science*: most "LLM-modified" text is language polishing, and every estimate is population-level and depends on a detector. Second, the fraud trend predates LLMs. Paper-mill output was already doubling about every 1.5 years against about 15 for the literature as a whole ([Richardson et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2420092122)), and "tortured phrases" from paraphrasing tools appeared in 2021 ([Cabanac et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.06751)). The strongest empirical counterweight is [Kusumegi et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adw3000). It finds productivity gains, especially for non-native English writers, and locates the harm in evaluators' reliance on proxies that AI cheapens.

## Section 3: AI tools help individuals but narrow the collective

The problem framing is robust. The burden of knowledge forces narrower specialisation and larger teams ([Jones 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-937X.2008.00531.x)), and interdisciplinary proposals have **consistently lower funding success** ([Bromham et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature18315)). That second finding means some barriers are institutional, not only cognitive. The solution evidence splits by architecture. General chatbots fabricate citations: GPT-4o did so in **78–90% of cases** on the OpenScholar benchmark. Retrieval-grounded open systems reach expert-level citation accuracy on curated benchmarks ([Asai et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-10072-4)). Validated discovery clusters where large curated data and automatic evaluators exist. AlphaFold is the only case with full peer review, broad uptake and a Nobel Prize.

The central empirical tension comes from Hao et al.: scientists who use AI publish **3.02× more papers** and receive **4.84× more citations**, while science's topical range shrinks and engagement between scientists falls **22%** ([Hao et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09922-y)). AI thus reproduces the publish-or-perish trade-off between individual gain and collective good. Chu & Evans imply a further paradox: AI that raises output without raising attention capacity worsens the deluge it promises to tame. Sourati & Evans show that design matters, because AI aimed at the "blind spots" between communities widens exploration ([Sourati & Evans 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-023-01648-z)).

## Section 4: reform machinery exists but is voluntary and pre-AI

No source located argues that research governance is adequately adapting to AI, and that absence is itself telling. Reform declarations have piled up for a decade: DORA, Leiden, the Metric Tide, the Hong Kong Principles, UNESCO and CoARA. They are voluntary and signatory-based. The European Commission's own June 2026 baseline study finds engagement "broad and increasing" but progress "uneven" ([EC/ERA 2026](https://european-research-area.ec.europa.eu/index.php/news/new-study-assesses-progress-reforming-research-assessment)). None of these instruments was written with machine-scale output in view. AI-specific governance is reactive, focused on disclosure and on the individual: it bans AI authorship ([Thorp 2023](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adg7879)) and restricts AI in review. It does not address what happens to a system that counts outputs once producing them costs almost nothing. In the UK, most universities still lack research-AI guidance ([HEPI 2025](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/2025/12/01/generative-ai-and-the-ref-closing-the-gap-between-policy-and-practice/)).

Three nuances keep this section honest. First, real alternatives exist and work where adopted. Registered Reports show **44% positive results against 96%** in the standard literature ([Scheel et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/25152459211007467)), and eLife, Utrecht and funding lotteries are working examples. These experiments are niche, though, or built *outside* incumbent institutions (FROs, Arc, ARIA). Second, the reform package has serious critics on both flanks. Scientometricians warn of "bibliometric denialism" ([Torres-Salinas et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-023-04787-2)) and Baccini argues that CoARA keeps evaluation administrative ([Baccini 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/reseval/rvaf024)). Third, the flagship alternative, narrative CVs, rests on thin evidence ([Bordignon et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/reseval/rvad013)) and may shift bias onto non-native English writers. Spain is a partial exception: ANECA's 2023 *sexenio* criteria include a required AI-use statement, though it too is a disclosure measure.

## Section 5: frontier capability is concentrated; "global mind" is a metaphor to defend

Concentration of frontier capability is well documented. Industry dominates compute, talent and the largest models ([Ahmed et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.ade2420)), and "open" branding rarely removes dependence on a few firms' infrastructure ([Widder et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08141-1)). The "harvesting through chats" claim has primary-source support. Anthropic's 2025 consumer-terms update made training on consumer chats the default unless users opt out, with five-year retention for those who allow it ([Anthropic 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/news/updates-to-our-consumer-terms)). (Disclosure: this report was compiled with an Anthropic model.) Stack Overflow activity fell after ChatGPT as knowledge exchange moved into private chats ([del Rio-Chanona et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae400)). Extraction also runs through publishers: Wiley expected **USD 44M** from AI licensing with "no specific opt-out for authors" ([The Bookseller](https://thebookseller.com/news/wiley-set-to-earn-44m-from-ai-rights-deals-confirms-no-opt-out-for-authors)).

The thesis needs three disciplines here. First, concentration applies to the *frontier*, not to all AI. Academia still produces the most highly cited AI research, and open-weight models narrowed the benchmark gap **from 8% to 1.7%** in a single year ([Stanford HAI 2025](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report)). Economists explain the concentration through scale economies rather than intent ([Korinek & Vipra 2025](https://doi.org/10.3386/w33139)). Second, monoculture risk comes from *shared* models whoever owns them ([Kleinberg & Raghavan 2021](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2018340118)), so open models alone do not cure homogenisation. Third, no peer-reviewed source uses "global mind controlled by a few firms." The nearest authoritative framing treats LLMs as *cultural and social technologies* that aggregate human knowledge, like markets or libraries, not as minds ([Farrell et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adt9819)).

## Where the thesis should be reframed

The evidence does not refute any of the five sections, but six claims need sharper wording to survive critical review.

| Thesis claim as framed | What the evidence shows | Suggested reframing |
|---|---|---|
| "AI is destroying the academic system" | Output growth (+47% in 2016–22), paper mills doubling every ~1.5 years, Hindawi's mass retractions and "tortured phrases" all predate LLMs (Hanson; Richardson; Cabanac; Van Noorden). Dey (2026) and Kusumegi (2025) argue AI *exposes* the flaws. | AI **amplifies and accelerates pre-existing flaws**. It collapses the cost of exactly the outputs the system counts (papers, citations, reviews, grant text), which makes an already unsustainable incentive model untenable. "Exposes" and "destroys" are compatible at the level of the system. |
| "The current system is broken" | Distortion is sharpest where incentives are crude: cash-per-paper, hard citation thresholds, volume-linked APC revenue (Fanelli 2015; Seeber; Baccini 2019). Stable incentives need not degrade quality (Korytkowski & Kulczycki; Osuna; Sandström). Fanelli (2018) rejects the "crisis" narrative. The disruption decline is contested (Petersen; Holst). | "Under measurable strain and **demonstrably gameable where incentives are crude**." Present Park et al. only together with its critics. |
| AI prevalence figures equal corrupted science | Estimates of 13.5–22% describe LLM-*modified text*, mostly polishing, and are population-level and detector-dependent. Vendor figures (Pangram, GPTZero) carry commercial interests. | Keep "AI text" separate from "AI-fabricated science." The sharpest evidence is **AI changing evaluation outcomes** (Latona) and funders rationing access (NIH cap, arXiv CS). |
| §3 "AI helps researchers stay knowledgeable" versus §5 "a few firms control the global mind" | The tools that best fight overload (OpenScholar, PaperQA2) need large corpora and compute. Proprietary chatbots fabricate 78–90% of citations. AI's individual gains come with collective narrowing (Hao), and monoculture arises from *shared* models even when they are open (Kleinberg & Raghavan). | Make the tension explicit: §3's benefits are real only for **open, retrieval-grounded, auditable tools** (OpenScholar, OLMo, Apertus; Palmer et al.), and only if designed for exploration (Sourati & Evans) rather than exploitation. Otherwise §3's remedy deepens §5's dependence and Chu & Evans's deluge. |
| §4 "institutions are not prepared" | No source claims governance is adapting adequately. But working reforms exist (Registered Reports, eLife, Utrecht, lotteries), and critics on both flanks dispute the reform package (Abramo; Torres-Salinas; Baccini). | Reform machinery is **voluntary, evidence-poor and designed before AI**, and AI governance is disclosure-centred. Genuine experiments sit at the margins or **outside incumbent institutions** (FROs, Arc, ARIA). |
| §5 "a global mind controlled by a few companies" | No peer-reviewed source uses this phrase. Farrell et al. call LLMs *cultural technologies*, not minds. Wells and Heylighen imagined a public, distributed world brain. Concentration is strongest at the frontier, and open models narrowed the gap to 1.7%. Publishers, not just chat logs, feed the extraction. | Label "global mind" as **the author's own metaphor**. Ground it as a *privatised, centralised version of the public world-brain ideal*, and state the empirical claim narrowly: **concentration of frontier capability, compute and new interaction data**, with publishers as co-beneficiaries. |

## Register of flagged works

Formal notices and disputed findings come first, because citing these without caveats would damage the paper's credibility. **Oviedo-García (2021)** on MDPI carries both an **expression of concern** and a **correction** ([rvab030](https://doi.org/10.1093/reseval/rvab030); [rvad014](https://doi.org/10.1093/reseval/rvad014)). **Sandström & Van den Besselaar (2018)** has a **corrigendum** ([joi.2024.101539](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joi.2024.101539)). **Toner-Rodgers (2024)** is **withdrawn** after MIT disavowed its data, and should appear only as a cautionary tale. **Park et al. (2023)** is **contested** by Petersen et al. and Holst et al. **Bloom et al. (2020)** may be contested by an NBER paper whose title alone was seen. The **Sakana Nature paper** concerns a high-acceptance workshop, not a main track. **Pangram** and **GPTZero** figures are vendor data, and Pangram's accuracy has been questioned.

Unverified details need a spot-check before quotation. These include all **PK**-flagged summaries and the Bornmann & Mutz (2015) growth rate, where the two research notes disagree; quote the 4.10% from the 2021 paper instead. Also unverified are Ioannidis et al.'s "~9,000 authors," Shen & Björk's predatory-OA volumes, and Kapoor & Narayanan's "17 fields." Hao et al.'s figures come from the arXiv abstract rather than the Nature text. The Robin Nature DOI, the EC 2026 baseline figures (secondary), and CASRAI's "78% pending" (do not use) are unconfirmed. Further unconfirmed items are the eLife JIF-loss claim, the EC living guidelines' 2025 edition, the ANECA reform (slides, not the BOE), Baccini's DOI (from arXiv metadata), the narrative-CV bias poster, and conflicting secondary dates for Anthropic's opt-out deadline. The SAPEA report, the DeepMind essay and the Kapoor & Narayanan 2025 essay were not read in full.

Some gaps remain open. The research found no peer-reviewed study of *sexenios* after ANECA's 2023 reform, and no causal study attributing conference submission growth to LLMs. It also found no outcome evaluation of DORA or CoARA hiring practices and no study linking chat-log harvesting to scientific homogenisation. Stephan's *How Economics Shapes Science* (2012), the CRediT/nanopublication literature, and OpenAI and Google consumer data policies were not verified.

## Conclusion

The literature turns the paper's thesis from a prediction into a diagnosis with a mechanism. Academic evaluation selects for countable outputs (Smaldino & McElreath), and generative AI has pushed the marginal cost of those outputs toward zero. The result is the very thing the reform movement has failed to dislodge for a decade: a system optimised for volume, now flooded with volume. The paper's most original contribution would be to show that §2 and §3 are the same phenomenon seen from two sides. Hao et al.'s finding of individual gain and collective contraction is publish-or-perish reproduced inside the tools. AI therefore improves science only where evaluation stops rewarding the quantity AI makes cheap.

That also links §4 and §5 more tightly than the outline suggests. Governance has answered AI with disclosure rules for individuals, while the infrastructure that would let science use AI on its own terms is being built privately. That infrastructure includes literature corpora that publishers license to developers, compute and interaction data. The actionable implication is that assessment reform and public, open AI infrastructure for science (OLMo, Apertus, OpenScholar-type tools) are one agenda, not two. The weakest move the paper could make is to lean on the "global mind" image without labelling it as metaphor. The strongest is to show that the narrower, evidenced claim about concentrated frontier capability and enclosed knowledge commons already carries the argument.
