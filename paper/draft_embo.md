# Rationing is not reform: academic evaluation after AI made writing cheap

**Academic evaluation read merit partly from what papers, grant applications and reviews cost to write. Generative AI makes that writing cheap, even when it is used honestly. Funders, preprint servers and conferences have responded by capping submissions, which restores scarcity but not the information it carried. Institutions should change what they count and how they read, and test whether AI is narrowing science.**

In July 2025 the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) reported that AI tools had let some investigators submit more than 40 applications in a single round, and it capped every investigator at six a year (NIH 2025). What AI had made cheap was the writing. A grant application is almost all writing; a wet-lab paper is mostly experiments, and its text is the part that AI cheapens. Other gatekeepers have followed. Since 1 October 2026 arXiv has accepted two submissions a month from each submitter (arXiv 2026), and AI conferences now cap submissions per author, two of them more tightly for authors without past acceptances (Singh Chawla 2026a). These are the responses with teeth so far, and they share a design: they restore scarcity by rule and leave what is counted unchanged. I argue that this is the wrong repair, because the damage does not depend on misuse. This essay was itself drafted by AI agents under my direction, in about three and a half hours of machine work from the start of the literature search to a referenced draft (Box 2), and nothing in its text shows that.

I write as a researcher in physics and biology, an insider to academic evaluation and an outsider to metascience. Much of the diagnosis is shared. Generative AI has been said to complete, and so destroy, publish-or-perish (Manabu 2026), to make surface polish an unreliable sign of merit (Hansen 2026) and, for biologists, to leave paper counts meaningless (Blekhman 2026). I build on Blekhman's argument and go beyond it in four ways. His case is the hollow paper, produced without research; mine is that real research loses its signal too. He predicts a fallback to journal prestige; the fallback already under way is rationing. He writes that the system has "no brakes"; the caps are brakes, crude ones. And I bring field-level data on where the surge is concentrated. Beyond Blekhman, I separate the drivers of any narrowing of science that may follow, and propose a test that could tell them apart (Box 1).

## The cost was the signal

Evaluation never read merit directly. Panels counted papers, grants and citations, and the counts worked, crudely, because what they counted was costly to produce. Three costs stood behind the outputs: the weeks of writing behind a paper or grant application, an expert's hours behind a review, and the years in a literature behind a credible synthesis. What carried information was the unevenness of these costs, since a strong application or a deep synthesis was hard for a weak producer to fake. Panels do more than read signals; they deliberate. But deliberation needs a manageable docket, and cost kept the docket manageable.

Generative AI lowers all three costs, and it does so when it is used honestly. At least 13.5% of PubMed abstracts from 2024 show signs of processing by large language models (LLMs), and up to about 40% in some groups (Kobak et al. 2025); most of this is ordinary use, such as language editing, that publishers permit. A study of 2.1 million preprints found that LLM use "reversed the relationship between writing complexity and paper quality" (Kusumegi et al. 2025). Reviews, which used to certify that an expert had spent time on a paper, are affected too: at machine-learning conferences, whose reviews are public, a substantial share are now classed as AI-generated (sources in the extended version; ref. 15). And the years of reading that a credible synthesis once displayed can now be imitated quickly; arXiv's computer-science section has stopped accepting review articles that have not passed peer review (Blekhman 2026). Summarising is not mastery, and judgement remains scarce. But judgement no longer shows in the text that used to display it.

Part of the loss is a gain. Fluent English partly signalled a native speaker, and scientists writing in a second language paid for that signal in time and effort (ref. 15). [AUTHOR: one sentence on your own experience as a non-native writer, if true.] The system is losing a partly unfair signal and has nothing yet to put in its place. Nor does the loss require hollow papers. A paper that reports months of genuine experiments, written up with AI help, looks no different from one whose authors wrote every line, and this essay, disclosed and accountable, says nothing in its text about the effort behind it. No rule about appropriate use can restore a signal that honest use removes.

Cheap writing also congests the gates, which is a separate claim, and the field data show where. In my companion analysis, new preprints in the four quarters to September 2026 exceeded 2022 levels by 50% on bioRxiv and by 139% on medRxiv once COVID-19 papers are removed, although medicine's 2022 base was depressed by the pandemic (Figure 1). In the last year biology grew by 10%, against 78% for arXiv mathematics, consistent with Blekhman's point that biology's bottlenecks are experimental. Mathematics shows how the growth is distributed. The share of new arXiv mathematics papers with an author who posted five or more in the same quarter rose from 5.3% in January 2025 to 19.3% in September 2026 (Figure 2). Matching authors by name inflates the level, as may posting finished papers in batches, but the share still at least doubled under stricter matching. The rise began in March 2026, seven months before arXiv's cap, and it came from hundreds of established mathematicians scaling up across team sizes and subfields, not from a handful of outliers. It is consistent with AI-assisted production, although only 2% of these papers mention an AI tool. Whatever its cause, a count whose heavy-producer share roughly triples within a year no longer measures what it did.

## Misuse is the visible tip

Misuse is real and well documented: paper mills, fabricated references, prompts hidden in manuscripts to steer AI reviewers and reviews written by machines (ref. 15 gives sources). Between misuse and honest use lies a grey zone. Single-factor association studies built on the US NHANES health survey rose from about four a year in 2014–21 to 190 in 2024, many with weak designs, a surge the authors link to AI-assisted production from ready-made datasets (Suchak et al. 2025). Nothing suggests that the prolific mathematicians misuse AI, but no gatekeeper reading the record could tell misuse from legitimate high productivity, and so institutions limit volume for everyone. Misuse is where the problem becomes visible, not where it ends.

## Rationing is not reform

By rationing I mean a limit on how many items a person may submit in a period, applied whatever the quality of the work or the conduct of its author. NIH's six applications a year, arXiv's two submissions a month and the conference quotas are rationing in this sense. arXiv's requirement that computer-science reviews first pass peer review is a gate, and its one-year bans for authors who do not check their AI output are sanctions aimed at misuse (Blekhman 2026). Most caps sit far above what typical researchers submit. NIH expects its cap to affect "a relatively small number of Principal Investigators" (NIH 2025), and even at the peak fewer than 3% of authors in arXiv mathematics posted five papers in a quarter. Rationing is not new either [CITATION NEEDED: e.g. a funder's long-standing limit on resubmissions or on applications per investigator]. What is new is the trigger, the reach to preprint servers and the tiering of quotas by past success.

A cap restores the scarcity of submissions and a little information with it, since an investigator limited to six applications reveals which six she thinks best. It cannot restore what costly production used to reveal across authors, and what is counted stays the same. Where quotas are tiered by past acceptances, past counted success decides who may be counted again: a Matthew effect written into the rules, to which critics object as a "perceived double standard" (Singh Chawla 2026a). Blekhman (2026) calls arXiv's measures reasonable but aimed at symptoms. I agree, and add that they are brakes on a system he believed had none, crude brakes applied by institutions that ration without being the ones that count. arXiv and conference committees cannot change what hiring panels reward. Universities, funders and assessment agencies can, and most have done neither. European funders, for instance, have issued guidance on how applicants and reviewers may use AI, but no caps and no change to what they count [AUTHOR: confirm for the ERC and your national funders before submission].

The next step is likely to be automation: detectors to police AI text, language models at the front line of review, and AI screening of submissions at inclusion, which Hansen (2026) proposes. If AI cheapened evaluation as fast as it cheapens production, volume would become manageable. But a judgement that a model makes about text a model helped to write measures what models find plausible, which is not the same as what a community has judged. Automated triage can sort for integrity and fit; it cannot supply the judgement whose scarcity the caps conceal.

The alternatives to rationing are older than AI. Fourie (2026) observes that review is "rationed, not priced", and fees or paid reviewing would price it, at a cost to poorer researchers. Hansen (2026) would publish all sound work and move prestige from rejection to curation, which still leaves the cheap output to be read; he names what remains, "the scarcity of interpretive attention". The reform movements are the natural allies. DORA and CoARA ask evaluators to read a researcher's work rather than count it, and a panel that reads three papers per candidate is not swamped when output doubles. [AUTHOR: say whether EMBL and UPF have signed DORA or CoARA; check before writing.] But reform depends on the resource that AI-era volume consumes. Without capacity for qualitative judgement, discretion migrates to whoever holds the gate, and machine-scale output uses up exactly that capacity. Rationing, meanwhile, keeps counts meaningful a little longer, which lowers the pressure to stop counting. That is why it is a fallback, already under way, and not a reform.

## Will cheap output narrow science?

A second worry is that AI will narrow what science studies. Huang et al. (2026) argue that "AI makes extending a familiar research line faster and cheaper than entering unfamiliar terrain, and existing incentive systems reward such speed". The best evidence is mixed. Across 41.3 million papers in six natural sciences, scientists who use AI as a research method publish 3.02 times as many papers and receive 4.84 times as many citations, while AI papers cover a 4.63% narrower range of topics (Hao et al. 2026). But that AI is mostly machine learning from before ChatGPT, the comparison is across papers rather than within scientists, and in the authors' supplementary analyses data availability "appears to be a major impacting factor" in where AI concentrates; incentives were not measured. Following 775,323 scientists, Zheng et al. (2026) find that after 2022 they increasingly published in intellectually distant fields and entered new ones. Both findings can hold if many individuals move into the same AI-amenable areas, each going further from their own past work while the diversity of the whole falls. Box 1 turns that reconciliation into a test.

Narrowing, if it comes, has separable drivers. The internal one is what researchers are rewarded for. The technology has its own pull towards well-posed, data-rich problems. And there is an external driver: a few firms build the tools, decide what they are trained on and learn from what users type. For biologists the sharpest form of this is confidentiality. Since 2023 the large AI assistants have converged on training on individual users' conversations by default, with an opt-out at most, while institutional accounts are excluded by contract (ref. 15 gives the companies' terms). The same researcher is outside the training data on a university licence and inside it on a personal account, with whatever unpublished results she has typed in.

## What AI could do

None of this is an argument against AI in science. Blekhman (2026) asks what biology research is for and answers with five goals: explaining living systems, improving health, building resources such as data, methods and model organisms, training the next generation and keeping the trust of the public that pays. In the world I would want, AI advances those goals, not the paper count, and keeps researchers literate in neighbouring fields without costing them depth in their own. Open, retrieval-grounded models already match human experts in citation accuracy on a benchmark, although their builders do not claim that such systems can fully automate synthesis (Asai et al. 2026). A synthesis whose every citation can be checked would lower the cost of reading, the scarcity that now matters most. That future needs tools that science can inspect and run itself, preferably open models hosted by institutions; this essay was made with a proprietary one, a compromise Box 2 explains.
