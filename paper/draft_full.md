# The cost was the signal: why academic evaluation is not ready for AI used well

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- Standfirst (excluded from the word count). Rewritten after the internal review: "legitimacy is irrelevant to the mechanism" became "the damage does not depend on misuse"; "almost free" now applies to the written layer only. -->
**Academic evaluation has long inferred merit partly from what its outputs cost to produce: the weeks behind a grant application, an expert's hours behind a review, the years of reading behind a synthesis. Generative AI lowers those costs even when it is used openly and well, so the outputs reveal less about the work behind them, and the damage does not depend on misuse. Institutions have answered mainly with caps on submissions, from NIH, arXiv and AI conferences, which restore the scarcity of submissions but not the information it carried. I argue that evaluation must change what it counts and how it reads, propose a test of whether incentives or the pull of data-rich problems would drive any narrowing of AI-era science, and document how this essay was itself made with AI agents.**

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶0.2 The claim, and the ground it shares (quotations paraphrased after the internal review) · 3,800: claim plus one sentence of shared premise -->
My claim is that academic evaluation is not prepared for a world in which AI is used generally. It infers merit partly from outputs that used to be costly to produce (Figure 1), and generative AI makes the written part of those outputs cheap even when it is used well. Much of this is shared ground. Early commentators expected AI to accelerate an existing overproduction of academic writing (Carrigan 2023) and to expose a system that was already failing (Kingsley 2023). Since then it has been argued that cheap AI output completes, and so destroys, publish-or-perish (Manabu 2026) [RECHECK], that polish no longer indicates merit (Hansen 2026), that AI-generated papers break the rough link between effort and value (Nature 2026) and that paper counts have become meaningless (Blekhman 2026). At one management journal, submissions rose by 42% after ChatGPT's release, and the editors attribute nearly all of the rise to AI-generated writing (Gartenberg et al. 2026) [RECHECK].

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶0.3 Contribution: each part names its closest precedent and the increment over it (internal review R1-1); Blekhman's differences made explicit (author's decision) -->
What I add is narrower, and each part has a close precedent. First, rationing. Blekhman (2026) predicts that evaluation will fall back on journal prestige; I document a different fallback that is already under way. The per-author limits that NIH, arXiv and AI conferences introduced in 2025–26 ration access to evaluation, much as journals ration their reviewers' attention (Fourie 2026) [RECHECK]. They restore the scarcity of submissions by rule but not the information that costly submissions carried, and automating the reading, the likely next step, does not restore it either. Second, the production side. Hanneke et al. (2026) name the scarce resources of evaluation; I argue that three costs of producing outputs also served as signals of merit and that honest use lowers all three, so that papers reporting real research lose the signal too, not only the hollow papers that concern Blekhman. Third, a test that could separate the incentive explanation of AI-era narrowing, argued by Huang et al. (2026) [RECHECK], from the view that AI simply goes where the data are. Fourth, I separate that internal driver from an external one: who builds the tools, on what data, and what they learn from their users (Figure 2). The essay also brings field-level data on the surge it discusses, and it is a documented case of the use it describes.

## 1. How the system works

<!-- CUT-3500 --><!-- ¶1.1 Counts stand in for hard-to-see qualities · Butler 2003 removed (memory-sourced and contested); quotations paraphrased -->
Evaluation judges qualities that are hard to see, such as originality and rigour, by counting what is easy to see: publication counts, impact factors and grant income dominate the criteria for hiring and promotion (Moher et al. 2018), and researchers respond to what is counted. Italy's citation thresholds for promotion were followed by a sharp rise in self-citation (Seeber et al. 2019); Chinese universities paid from about US$30 to US$165,000 for a paper in a Web of Science journal (Quan et al. 2017) [RECHECK]; and selection on counts can favour weak methods without anyone cheating (Smaldino & McElreath 2016). The counter-evidence matters. Fanelli (2018) rejects the narrative of a science in crisis [RECHECK], diffuse pressure to publish does not predict retractions although cash rewards do (Fanelli et al. 2015), and stable Polish incentives raised output with no measured fall in quality (Korytkowski & Kulczycki 2019) [RECHECK]. The system was measurably strained before AI, and gameable where its incentives were crude.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶1.2 Pre-AI strain; Park et al. now only as a clause, with its critics (brief §6) · 3,800: one clause (Hanson et al.) -->
Before ChatGPT, output was already outgrowing the capacity to read and review it. Indexed articles were about 47% more numerous in 2022 than in 2016, outpacing the growth in practising scientists (Hanson et al. 2024) [RECHECK]. Peer review took more than 100 million reviewer-hours in 2020 (Aczel et al. 2021), and an Australian grant proposal took 34 working days to prepare, for a 21% chance of funding (Herbert et al. 2013). In large fields, the flood of papers slows the entry of new ideas into the canon (Chu & Evans 2021). Growth itself is old, about 4% a year (Bornmann et al. 2021), and reviewer fatigue may be overstated (Breuning et al. 2015); the reported decline in "disruptive" papers (Park et al. 2023) rests on a disputed measure (Petersen et al. 2024; Holst et al. 2026), and nothing here depends on it. The gates were already the binding constraint, so anything that makes production cheap reaches them first.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶1.3 The gates worked because outputs were costly (pivot paragraph) · adds differential cost (R1-2b), panels as deliberation (R2-8) and Adeyeye (R1-1); the distinction from Hansen is now stated only here (R2-7d) -->
Why did the gates work at all? Because what they counted was costly to produce, and the cost carried information. Three costs stood behind the outputs: the effort of writing papers and grant applications, an expert's time behind each review, and years in a literature behind a credible synthesis. I will call them the three scarcities. What carried information was less the cost than its unevenness: "signals are often informative insofar as they require differential knowledge and effort to produce" (Cowgill et al. 2026). Blekhman (2026) traces paper-based evaluation to the same fact, Adeyeye (2026) [RECHECK] applies signalling theory to the publication itself, and arXiv now says that before AI there was a practical limit to how fast submissions could be produced (arXiv 2026). Panels do more than read signals; they deliberate and negotiate what excellence means [CITATION NEEDED: Lamont 2009, *How Professors Think*; suggested by the internal review, not in the notes]. But deliberation needs a manageable docket, and cost kept it manageable. I do not defend the proxies, which were poor guides to individual merit long before AI (Seglen 1997; Brembs et al. 2013); the system relied on them, and they are failing faster than replacements appear. Nor is this Hansen's (2026) symbolic scarcity, the prestige that rejection produces, which he rightly calls unnecessary for knowledge, although his remark that surface quality no longer indicates merit is the signal argument in brief. The scarcity I mean lay in costly inputs, and however crudely, it did epistemic work.

## 2. AI makes what the system counts cheap to write

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.1 Which layer is cheap (internal review E3); use is broad · Suchak et al. moved to ¶2.9 · 3,800: drop the last two sentences -->
Generative AI has cut the cost of the written layer of every counted output. That layer is the whole of a grant application, a review or a review article, but only part of an experimental paper, whose cost lies mostly in the experiments. Use is broad: at least 13.5% of 2024 PubMed abstracts show signs of processing by large language models (LLMs), up to about 40% in some subgroups (Kobak et al. 2025), as do up to 22% of sentences in computer-science abstracts (Liang et al. 2025). These estimates rest on detectors, which misfire on non-native English writers (Liang et al. 2023) and measure style rather than purpose. What they establish is that use is common, and much of it, such as language editing, is permitted; Springer Nature does not even ask authors to declare AI-assisted copy editing (Nature Portfolio n.d.). Readers who know Kobak's marker words may search this essay for them. If they find none, they will have learned nothing about how it was written.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.2 Signal 1, writing (core of contribution 1) · Cowgill now leads (R1-4g); Kusumegi's output estimate reported as contested and the non-native proxy stated (audit R06–R09) -->
Take writing first. Once fluent prose is cheap for everyone, it says less about the research behind it. In a study of entrepreneurship and hiring, giving senders ChatGPT lowered the accuracy with which they were screened by 4–9%, though for senders from non-English-speaking countries accuracy rose (Cowgill et al. 2026); on a freelancing platform, employers stopped paying a premium for customised applications once LLMs arrived (Galdin & Silbert 2025) [RECHECK]. In 2.1 million preprints, LLM use "reversed the relationship between writing complexity and paper quality" (Kusumegi et al. 2025) [RECHECK]. The same study's estimate that adopters raised their output by 23.7–89.3% is contested by a peer-reviewed comment, which shows that its way of dating adoption can produce spurious gains by itself (Renault et al. 2026) [RECHECK]. Part of the loss is a gain. Polished English partly signalled a native speaker, at a cost that non-native scientists paid in time and effort (Amano et al. 2023), and Kusumegi et al. estimated the largest gains for authors with Asian names at Asian institutions, their proxy for non-native writers [RECHECK]. The system is losing a partly unfair signal and has nothing yet to put in its place. [AUTHOR: if true, one sentence saying that you, as a non-native writer, benefit from this yourself.]

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.3 Signal 2, reviewer time · machine-learning venues named as such (R1-7); Thakkar et al. reported as "updated", not "improved" (R1 minor 5); the "suspected AI review" slot was cut (R2-7g) · 3,800: drop the trial sentence -->
Reviews matter more, because they decide outcomes, and a review used to certify that an expert had spent time on a paper. Most of the evidence comes from machine-learning conferences, whose reviews are public. At ICLR 2024 at least 15.8% of reviews were AI-assisted, and borderline papers that received one were 4.9 percentage points more likely to be accepted (Russo et al. 2025) [RECHECK]; for 2025, a detector classes about 20% of ICLR reviews and 12% of *Nature Communications* reviews as AI-generated (Shen & Wang 2026) [RECHECK]. Assistance can also be open and deliberate: when an AI agent commented on more than 20,000 ICLR 2025 reviews, 27% of the reviewers who received its feedback updated their reviews (Thakkar et al. 2025) [RECHECK]. Whatever its quality, an assisted review no longer shows that an expert spent time on the paper, and cheap reviews now meet cheap papers.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.4 Signal 1 again, in grants · "binds" corrected to "applies" (R1-3a); no longer implies that NIH uses detectors (R1 minor 6) -->
Grant applications used to be rationed by the weeks they took to write. In July 2025 the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) reported that "the use of AI tools has enabled Principal Investigators to submit more than 40 distinct applications in a single application submission round". It now accepts at most six applications per investigator a year and no longer treats applications "substantially developed by AI" as original (NIH 2025); Parrilla (2023) had already argued that ChatGPT's usefulness showed the grant system to be broken. Forty applications in a round looks like abuse, and the cap is set where only such outliers reach it. What makes it rationing rather than a sanction is that it applies to every investigator, whatever their conduct. Enforcing the originality rule means telling AI-developed text from human text, which detectors do unreliably: they misfire on non-native writers (Liang et al. 2023) and drift as authors drop the words that give them away (Geng & Trotta 2025) [RECHECK].

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.5 Signal 3, mastery time: the scarcity nobody counted · labelled as conceptual (R1-7); arXiv's rule shows that synthesis-shaped texts got cheap, not mastery (R1 minor 7) -->
The third scarcity was never counted, yet gatekeepers leaned on it. A credible synthesis, whether a review article or a background section that got the history right, showed years spent in a literature. The claim is conceptual; I know of no study that measures how far evaluators relied on it. AI shortens that time, lowering what Huang et al. (2026) [RECHECK] call the informational cost of pivoting, and retrieval-grounded systems now cite about as accurately as experts on one benchmark (Asai et al. 2026). Texts shaped like syntheses have certainly become cheap: in October 2025 arXiv's computer-science section, faced with hundreds of review articles a month, stopped accepting reviews and position papers that had not passed peer review (arXiv 2025). Summarising is not mastery, and judgement remains scarce: "Writing a plausible manuscript is now largely rule-based …; judging one is not" (Fourie 2026) [RECHECK]. But the judgement is now invisible in the text that used to display it, so the change that makes Section 3's ideal possible also removes a signal. [AUTHOR: an example from your own field, e.g. a background section on an unfamiliar area that an agent drafted quickly, and what in it you could not yet judge.]

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.7 The information mechanism (R1-2: split from congestion; "invisible", not "gone"); Blekhman's hollow papers vs real research (author's decision) -->
With all three costs lowered, what the system counts reveals less about the work behind it, whoever answers for the output. The signals have not disappeared: judgement and mastery remain scarce, and some writers still write better than others. But AI narrows the differences in cost on which the signals relied, apparently most for less fluent writers, so a fluent paper, a competent review or a well-cited synthesis separates less clearly those who did the work from those who did less of it. Blekhman's (2026) case is the plausible paper produced without any research. Mine is the paper that reports real research and still loses its signal, because the effort behind it has become invisible. This holds whether or not the use is disclosed. Gartenberg et al. (2026) decline to say what level of AI use is appropriate; my point is that no rule about appropriate use restores the signal. Nor does accountability, whose preservation Braun (2026) calls the deeper challenge: it says who answers for a paper and nothing about the effort or judgement in it, and disclosure alone rarely shows how AI-assisted work was done (Cwik 2026). This essay is disclosed and accountable, and its text still says nothing about the effort behind it.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.8 (was ¶2.6b) The congestion mechanism, held conditionally · companion-analysis figures added at the coordinator's request (Figures 3 and 4); Blekhman's experimental-bottleneck point; Tao 2026 · 3,800: keep the mathematics sentences and the last three sentences -->
The second mechanism is congestion, cheap output overloading gates of fixed capacity, and its case is real but narrower. New preprints rose in every field my companion analysis examined; in the twelve months to September 2026 they exceeded 2022 levels by 44% in physics, 50% in biology, 59% in mathematics and 146% in computer science (Figure 3) [RECHECK: companion analysis, pending confirmation]. Year on year, biology grew by 10% and mathematics by 78%, consistent with Blekhman's (2026) point that biology's bottlenecks are experimental. The growth is also concentrated. In arXiv's mathematics section, the share of new papers with an author who posted five or more mathematics papers that quarter stayed between 4% and 7% until March 2026, then reached 19.3% in September, when 8,360 papers appeared against 3,197 in January 2025 (Figure 4) [RECHECK: companion analysis, pending confirmation; robustness checks pending]. That is consistent with AI-assisted production, which mathematicians are already debating (Tao 2026) [RECHECK], but it is not proof: only 2% of these papers mention an AI tool, and because authors are matched by name, namesakes, larger teams and batch posting could inflate the share. An independent estimate points the same way: arXiv's 2026 mathematics volume ran 11.9% above a synthetic counterfactual, and the share of authors with five or more submissions rose from 2.45% to 3.80% (Nee et al. 2026) [RECHECK]. The load comes largely from a tail of heavy producers, as the institutions themselves say (NIH 2025; arXiv 2026). For the information argument the tail's legitimacy is beside the point: a count whose heavy-producer share roughly triples within a year no longer measures what it did, and the tail is where cheap production first becomes visible. For congestion it is not beside the point. No one has shown that ordinary use alone would overload a gate, so I hold that claim conditionally.

### Misuse is the visible tip

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.9 New in draft 2 (author's decision, 16 Oct 2026; replaces ¶2.6, fraud as amplifier). Misuse is well researched, so one clause each, not a review. Never imply that the prolific authors in the mathematics data misuse AI. · 3,800: first and last three sentences -->
Misuse is real and well documented. Paper mills were outgrowing the literature long before ChatGPT (Richardson et al. 2025); hallucinated references and leftover chatbot text prompted arXiv's one-year bans (Section 4c); authors have hidden prompts in manuscripts to steer AI reviewers (Lin 2025) [RECHECK]; and some reviews are generated by AI (Shen & Wang 2026). Between misuse and legitimate use lies a grey zone of formulaic output. Single-factor association studies built on the US NHANES health survey rose from about four a year in 2014–21 to 190 in 2024; many have weak designs, and the authors link the surge to AI-assisted, paper-mill-style production from AI-ready datasets (Suchak et al. 2025). The template needs no fraud, only a public dataset and a language model, and where the data come ready-made the whole paper becomes cheap, not just its prose.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.10 New in draft 2 (author's decision): why the distinction does not help gatekeepers; leads into Section 4c -->
The mathematics data show why the distinction does not help gatekeepers. Nothing in them suggests that the prolific authors misuse AI; the point is that no one, reading the record, could split the rise in heavy producers into misuse and legitimate high productivity, and detectors cannot settle it either (¶2.4). So institutions limit volume for everyone, the subject of Section 4c. Misuse is where the problem becomes visible, not where it ends. Remove every paper mill, fabricated reference and machine-written review, and the effort behind honest work is still invisible in the text.
