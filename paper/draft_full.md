# The cost was the signal: why academic evaluation is not ready for AI used well

**Academic evaluation has long inferred merit partly from what its outputs cost to produce: the weeks behind a grant application, an expert's hours behind a review, the years of reading behind a synthesis. Generative AI lowers those costs even when it is used openly and well, so the outputs reveal less about the work behind them, and the damage does not depend on misuse. Institutions have answered mainly with caps on submissions, from NIH, arXiv and AI conferences, which restore the scarcity of submissions but not the information it carried. I argue that evaluation must change what it counts and how it reads, propose a test of whether incentives or the pull of data-rich problems would drive any narrowing of AI-era science, and document how this essay was itself made with AI agents.**

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶0.2 The claim, and the ground it shares (quotations paraphrased after the internal review) · 3,800: claim plus one sentence of shared premise -->
My claim is that academic evaluation is not prepared for a world in which AI is used generally. It infers merit partly from outputs that used to be costly to produce (Figure 1), and generative AI makes the written part of those outputs cheap even when it is used well. Much of this is shared ground. Early commentators expected AI to accelerate an existing overproduction of academic writing (Carrigan 2023) and to expose a system that was already failing (Kingsley 2023). Since then it has been argued that cheap AI output completes, and so destroys, publish-or-perish (Manabu 2026) [RECHECK], that polish no longer indicates merit (Hansen 2026), that AI-generated papers break the rough link between effort and value (Nature 2026) and that paper counts have become meaningless (Blekhman 2026). At one management journal, submissions rose by 42% after ChatGPT's release, and the editors attribute nearly all of the rise to AI-generated writing (Gartenberg et al. 2026) [RECHECK].

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶0.3 Contribution: each part names its closest precedent and the increment over it (internal review R1-1) · 3,800: keep -->
What I add is narrower, and each part has a close precedent. First, rationing. Fourie (2026) [RECHECK] argues that journal review is "rationed, not priced", a rationing of evaluators' attention. I read the per-author limits that NIH, arXiv and AI conferences introduced in 2025–26 as a rationing of access to evaluation: they restore the scarcity of submissions by rule but not the information that costly submissions carried, and the step institutions are likely to take next, automating the reading, does not restore it either. Second, the production side. Hanneke et al. (2026) name the scarce resources of evaluation: editorial attention, reviewer time and audience attention. I argue that three costs of producing outputs also served as signals of merit, that honest use lowers all three, and that one of them, the years it takes to master a literature, was never counted at all. Third, a test. Huang et al. (2026) [RECHECK] argue that AI speeds up established lines of work because institutions reward speed; I propose a design that could separate that explanation from the rival view that AI simply goes where the data are. Fourth, I separate an internal driver of any narrowing, what researchers are rewarded for, from an external one: who builds the tools, on what data, and what they learn from their users (Figure 2). The essay is also a documented case of the use it describes.

## 1. How the system works

<!-- CUT-3500 --><!-- ¶1.1 Counts stand in for hard-to-see qualities, and people respond to what is counted · Butler 2003 removed (memory-sourced and contested); quotations paraphrased -->
Evaluation judges qualities that are hard to see, such as originality and rigour, by counting what is easy to see. Publication counts, impact factors and grant income dominate the criteria that institutions use for hiring and promotion (Moher et al. 2018), and researchers respond to what is counted. After Italy introduced citation thresholds for promotion, self-citation rose sharply (Seeber et al. 2019). Chinese universities paid cash for papers in Web of Science journals, at rates from about US$30 to US$165,000 a paper (Quan et al. 2017) [RECHECK]. Selection on counts can even favour weak methods without anyone cheating (Smaldino & McElreath 2016). The counter-evidence matters, though. Fanelli (2018) rejects the narrative of a science in crisis [RECHECK]; diffuse pressure to publish does not predict retractions, although cash rewards do (Fanelli et al. 2015); and stable Polish incentives raised output with no measured fall in quality (Korytkowski & Kulczycki 2019) [RECHECK]. What can be defended is that the system was measurably strained before AI, and gameable where its incentives were crude.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶1.2 Pre-AI strain; Park et al. now only as a clause, with its critics (brief §6) · 3,800: one clause (Hanson et al.) -->
Before ChatGPT, output was already outgrowing the capacity to read and review it. Indexed articles were about 47% more numerous in 2022 than in 2016, outpacing the growth in practising scientists (Hanson et al. 2024) [RECHECK]. Peer review took more than 100 million reviewer-hours in 2020 (Aczel et al. 2021), and an Australian grant proposal took 34 working days to prepare, for a 21% chance of funding (Herbert et al. 2013). In large fields, the flood of papers slows the entry of new ideas into the canon (Chu & Evans 2021). Growth itself is old, about 4% a year (Bornmann et al. 2021), and reviewer fatigue may be overstated (Breuning et al. 2015). The reported decline in "disruptive" papers (Park et al. 2023) rests on a measure whose behaviour is disputed (Petersen et al. 2024; Holst et al. 2026), and nothing here depends on it. The gates were already the binding constraint, so anything that makes production cheap reaches them first.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶1.3 The gates worked because outputs were costly (pivot paragraph) · adds differential cost (R1-2b), panels as deliberation (R2-8), Adeyeye (R1-1); the Hansen distinction is now stated only here -->
Why did the gates work at all? Because what they counted was costly to produce, and the cost carried information. Three costs stood behind the outputs: the effort of writing papers and grant applications, which at 34 working days a proposal limited how many one person could send; an expert's time behind each review; and years in a literature behind a credible synthesis. I will call these the three scarcities. What carried information was less the cost than its unevenness: "signals are often informative insofar as they require differential knowledge and effort to produce" (Cowgill et al. 2026). Blekhman (2026) traces paper-based evaluation to the same fact, Adeyeye (2026) [RECHECK] applies signalling theory to the publication itself, and arXiv now says that before AI there was a practical limit to how fast submissions could be produced (arXiv 2026). Evaluation is more than the reading of signals; panels deliberate, weigh plural criteria and negotiate what excellence means [CITATION NEEDED: Lamont 2009, *How Professors Think*; suggested by the internal review, not in the notes]. But deliberation needs a manageable docket, and cost kept it manageable. I do not defend the proxies, which were poor guides to individual merit long before AI (Seglen 1997; Brembs et al. 2013). The system relied on them, and they are failing faster than replacements appear. Nor is this the scarcity that Hansen (2026) attacks. His is symbolic, the prestige that rejection produces, which he rightly calls unnecessary for knowledge, and his remark that surface quality no longer indicates merit is the signal argument in brief. The scarcity I mean lay in costly inputs. However crudely, it did epistemic work.

## 2. AI makes what the system counts cheap to write

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.1 Which layer is cheap; use is broad; Suchak et al. moved here from the fraud paragraph (internal review E3) · 3,800: drop the last two sentences -->
Generative AI has cut the cost of the written layer of every counted output. That layer is the whole of a grant application, a review or a review article, but only part of an experimental paper, whose cost lies mostly in the experiments. Use is broad. At least 13.5% of 2024 PubMed abstracts show signs of processing by large language models (LLMs), up to about 40% in some subgroups (Kobak et al. 2025), as do up to 22% of sentences in computer-science abstracts (Liang et al. 2025) and 6.5–16.9% of review text at four AI conferences (Liang et al. 2024). These estimates rest on detectors, which misfire on non-native English writers (Liang et al. 2023) and measure style rather than purpose; they cannot tell polishing from generation. What they establish is that use is common, and much of it, such as language editing, is permitted: Springer Nature does not even ask authors to declare AI-assisted copy editing (Nature Portfolio n.d.). Where the data come ready-made, the whole paper can become cheap. Single-factor association studies built on the US NHANES health survey rose from about four a year in 2014–21 to 190 in 2024; many have weak designs, and the authors link the surge to AI-assisted, paper-mill-style production from AI-ready datasets (Suchak et al. 2025). The template itself needs no misconduct, only a public dataset and a language model. Readers who know Kobak's marker words may search this essay for them. If they find none, they will have learned nothing about how it was written.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.2 Signal 1, writing (core of contribution 1) · Cowgill now leads; Kusumegi's output estimate reported as contested (audit R06–R09) -->
Take writing first. Once fluent prose is cheap for everyone, it says less about the research behind it. In a study of entrepreneurship and hiring, giving senders access to ChatGPT lowered the accuracy with which their messages were screened by 4–9%, although for senders from non-English-speaking countries accuracy rose (Cowgill et al. 2026). On a freelancing platform, employers stopped paying a premium for customised applications once LLMs arrived (Galdin & Silbert 2025) [RECHECK]. In 2.1 million preprints, LLM use "reversed the relationship between writing complexity and paper quality" (Kusumegi et al. 2025) [RECHECK]; the same study's estimate that adopters raised their output by 23.7–89.3% is contested by a peer-reviewed comment, which shows that its way of dating adoption can by itself produce spurious productivity gains (Renault et al. 2026) [RECHECK]. Journal editors report that simple prompting undermines writing quality as a screening signal (Hanneke et al. 2026). Part of the loss is a gain. Polished English partly signalled a native speaker, at a cost that non-native scientists paid in time and effort (Amano et al. 2023), and Kusumegi et al. estimated the largest gains for authors with Asian names at Asian institutions, their proxy for non-native writers [RECHECK]. The system is losing a partly unfair signal and has nothing yet to put in its place. [AUTHOR: if true, one sentence saying that you, as a non-native writer, benefit from this yourself.]

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.3 Signal 2, reviewer time · 3,800: drop the trial sentence; the "suspected AI review" slot was cut (internal review R2-7g) -->
Reviews matter more, because they decide outcomes. A review used to certify that an expert had spent time on a paper. Most of the evidence comes from machine-learning conferences, whose reviews are public. At ICLR 2024 at least 15.8% of reviews were AI-assisted, and borderline papers that received one were 4.9 percentage points more likely to be accepted (Russo et al. 2025) [RECHECK]; for 2025, a detector puts the share of AI-generated reviews at about 20% at ICLR and 12% at *Nature Communications* (Shen & Wang 2026) [RECHECK]. Assisted reviewing can also be done in the open: ICLR 2025 trialled an AI agent that commented on more than 20,000 reviews, and 27% of the reviewers who received its feedback updated their reviews (Thakkar et al. 2025) [RECHECK]. Whatever its quality, an assisted review no longer shows that an expert spent time on the paper, and cheap reviews now meet cheap papers.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.4 Signal 1 again, in grants · "binds" corrected to "applies" (R1-3a); the detector point no longer implies that NIH uses detectors -->
Grant applications used to be rationed by the weeks they took to write. In July 2025 the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) reported that "the use of AI tools has enabled Principal Investigators to submit more than 40 distinct applications in a single application submission round". It now accepts at most six applications per investigator a year and no longer treats applications "substantially developed by AI" as original (NIH 2025), as Parrilla (2023) had foreshadowed when he argued that ChatGPT's usefulness showed the grant system to be broken. Forty applications in a round looks like abuse, and the cap is set where only such outliers reach it. What makes it rationing and not a sanction is that it applies to every investigator, whatever their conduct (Section 4c). Enforcing the originality rule, meanwhile, means telling AI-developed text from human text, which detectors do unreliably: they misfire on non-native writers (Liang et al. 2023) and drift as authors drop the words that give them away (Geng & Trotta 2025) [RECHECK].

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.5 Signal 3, mastery time: the scarcity nobody counted · labelled as conceptual (R1-7); arXiv's rule shows that synthesis-shaped texts got cheap, not mastery (R1 minor 7) -->
The third scarcity was never counted, yet gatekeepers leaned on it. A credible synthesis, whether a review article or a background section that got the history right, showed years spent in a literature. The claim is conceptual; I know of no study that measures how far evaluators relied on it. AI shortens that time. It lowers what Huang et al. (2026) [RECHECK] call the informational cost of pivoting, and retrieval-grounded systems now cite about as accurately as experts on one benchmark (Asai et al. 2026). Texts shaped like syntheses have certainly become cheap: in October 2025 arXiv's computer-science section stopped accepting review and position papers that had not passed peer review, after receiving hundreds of review articles a month (arXiv 2025). Manabu (2026) sees the apprenticeship through which scholars were formed being cut short. Summarising is not mastery, and judgement remains scarce: "Writing a plausible manuscript is now largely rule-based …; judging one is not" (Fourie 2026) [RECHECK]. But judgement is now invisible in the text that used to display it, so the change that makes the ideal of Section 3 possible also removes a signal. [AUTHOR: an example from your own field, e.g. a background section on an unfamiliar area that an agent drafted quickly, and what in it you could not yet judge.]

<!-- CUT-3500 --><!-- ¶2.6 Fraud amplifies but is not the core · Suchak et al. moved to ¶2.1 · 3,800: one sentence -->
Fraud amplifies all this but is not the core, and it predates LLMs: suspected fraudulent output was doubling about every 1.5 years, against about 15 for the literature as a whole (Richardson et al. 2025). Better detection would address this amplifier and leave untouched the cheap, legitimate output that this essay is about.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.7 The information mechanism: the signal is weakened and the effort invisible, whatever the conduct (R1-2: split from congestion; "invisible", not "gone") -->
With all three costs lowered, what the system counts reveals less about the work behind it, whoever answers for the output. The signals have not disappeared. Judgement and mastery remain scarce, and some writers still write better than others. What AI does is narrow the differences in cost on which the signals relied, and the evidence above suggests it narrows them most for less fluent writers, so a fluent paper, a competent review or a well-cited synthesis separates less clearly those who did the work from those who did less of it. The effort has become invisible, which is different from unnecessary. This holds whether or not the use is disclosed, and so the remedies on offer do not reach it. Gartenberg et al. (2026) decline to say what level of AI use is appropriate; my point is that no rule about appropriate use restores the signal. Nor does accountability, whose preservation Braun (2026) calls the deeper challenge. Accountability says who answers for a paper and nothing about the effort or judgement in it, and disclosure alone rarely shows how AI-assisted work was done (Cwik 2026). arXiv allows AI use as long as it is disclosed, and its cap applies to the disclosed and the undisclosed alike (arXiv 2026). This essay is disclosed and accountable, and its text still says nothing about the effort behind it.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.8 (was ¶2.6b) The congestion mechanism, held conditionally; companion-analysis figures added at the coordinator's request (Figures 3 and 4) · 3,800: keep the mathematics sentences and the last three sentences -->
The second mechanism is congestion, cheap output overloading gates of fixed capacity, and the case for it is real but narrower. In every field my companion analysis examined, new preprints rose: in the twelve months to September 2026, compared with 2022, by 44% in physics, 50% in biology, 59% in mathematics and 146% in computer science (Figure 3) [RECHECK: companion analysis, pending confirmation]. The growth is concentrated. In arXiv's mathematics section, the share of new papers with an author who posted five or more mathematics papers in the same quarter stayed between 4% and 7% until March 2026 and reached 19.3% in September, when 8,360 mathematics papers appeared against 3,197 in January 2025 (Figure 4) [RECHECK: companion analysis, pending confirmation; robustness checks pending]. The pattern is consistent with AI-assisted production, although only 2% of these papers mention an AI tool, and because the measure matches authors by name, namesakes, larger teams and batch posting could inflate it. A separate study finds the same direction by another route: arXiv's 2026 mathematics volume ran 11.9% above a synthetic counterfactual, and the share of authors with five or more submissions rose from 2.45% to 3.80% (Nee et al. 2026) [RECHECK]. So the load comes largely from a tail of heavy producers, which is also what the institutions say. NIH acted on investigators with more than 40 applications in a round, and arXiv's announcement quotes the chair of its computer-science section on "a relatively small proportion of authors" submitting large numbers of weak papers (arXiv 2026) [RECHECK]. These data cannot say whether that tail is misuse, legitimate cheap production or an artefact of name matching. For the information argument it does not matter: a count whose heavy-producer share roughly triples within a year no longer measures what it measured before, and the tail is where cheap production first becomes visible, whatever its legitimacy. For congestion it matters a great deal. No one has shown that ordinary use alone would overload a gate, and if the tail carried the whole load, targeted sanctions would be the better tool. I therefore hold the information claim firmly and the congestion claim conditionally.
