# Even good use breaks it: academic evaluation is not prepared for AI

**[AUTHOR: name]**¹˒²

¹ European Molecular Biology Laboratory (EMBL), [AUTHOR: site]. ² Universitat Pompeu Fabra (UPF), Barcelona, Spain.
Correspondence: [AUTHOR: email]

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- Standfirst (excluded from the word count) · EMBO version: cut to about 60 words -->
**Generative AI makes papers, reviews and grant applications almost free to produce, and it does so when it is used well, not only when it is abused. Academic evaluation, which reads merit from outputs that used to be costly, is not prepared for this. I argue that legitimacy is irrelevant to the mechanism: honest use erodes the three scarcities on which the signals rest (writing effort, reviewers' time and the time it takes to master a literature). Institutions have so far responded by rationing rather than reform. NIH caps applications, arXiv caps submissions and AI conferences cap authors, restoring scarcity by decree while leaving what is counted unchanged. I distinguish an internal driver of the narrowing that may follow (metric incentives) from an external one (corporate control of tools and chat data), propose a test that discriminates between incentives and the pull of data-rich problems, and document how this essay was itself made with AI agents.**

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶0.2 The claim, and the ground it shares · 3,500: the claim plus one sentence of shared premise citing Blekhman and Hansen; Manabu and Gartenberg move to ¶0.3 -->
My claim is that academic evaluation is not prepared for a world in which AI is used generally. It reads merit from outputs that used to be costly to produce, and generative AI makes them almost free even when it is used legitimately. Much of this is shared ground. Early commentary expected AI to speed up academic overproduction (Carrigan 2023) and saw ChatGPT 'holding a mirror' to an ailing system (Kingsley 2023). By 2026, publish-or-perish was being 'completed, and thereby destroyed' by large language models (LLMs) (Manabu 2026) [RECHECK]; 'surface quality no longer reliably indicates epistemic merit' (Hansen 2026); AI-generated papers 'shred the long-standing (if rough) correlation between authors applying effort and the work having value' (Nature 2026); and 'paper counts are meaningless' (Blekhman 2026). At one journal, submissions rose 42% after ChatGPT's release, nearly all of it attributable to AI-generated writing (Gartenberg et al. 2026; see also Lang et al. 2025; Arndt & Zhang 2026; Dey 2026).

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶0.3 Contribution · EMBO version: 60–80 words listing the four contributions and the record -->
What I add is narrower. Recent papers argue that generative AI strains academic evaluation by making its outputs cheap (Manabu 2026; Hansen 2026; Blekhman 2026), and Huang et al. (2026) [RECHECK] that it accelerates established lines of work rather than opening new ones. I build on them in four ways. First, I locate the failure in legitimate use: signals of merit rest on three scarcities (writing effort, reviewers' time and the time it takes to master a literature), and honest use erodes all three (cf. Hanneke et al. 2026). Second, I propose a test that discriminates between the incentive explanation of narrowing and the rival view that AI simply goes where the data are, which I plan to run as a preregistered study [AUTHOR: confirm, or delete this clause]. Third, extending Fourie's (2026, preprint) [RECHECK] observation that review is 'rationed, not priced', I read NIH's application cap, arXiv's 2025–26 rules and conference quotas together as rationing that restores scarcity by decree. Fourth, I separate an internal driver of narrowing (incentives) from an external one (corporate control of tools and chat data). The essay is also a documented case of the use it describes.

## 1. How the system works

<!-- CUT-3500 --><!-- ¶1.1 Counts stand in for hard-to-see qualities, and people respond to whatever is counted -->
Evaluation has to judge qualities that are hard to see, such as originality and rigour, so it counts what is easy to see: publication counts, impact factors and grant income dominate the criteria for hiring and promotion (Moher et al. 2018). Researchers respond to the count as it is designed. Australia's count-based funding formula raised its share of indexed papers while their relative impact fell (Butler 2003) [RECHECK]; Italy's citation thresholds brought 'a strong and significant increase in self-citations' (Seeber et al. 2019); Chinese universities paid up to US$165,000 per Web of Science paper (Quan et al. 2017); and selection on counts can favour weak methods without anyone cheating (Smaldino & McElreath 2016). The counter-evidence matters. Fanelli (2018) calls the crisis narrative 'mistaken'; diffuse pressure to publish does not predict retractions, though cash rewards do (Fanelli et al. 2015); and stable Polish incentives raised output with no measured fall in quality (Korytkowski & Kulczycki 2019) [RECHECK]. The defensible claim is that the system was measurably strained, and gameable where its incentives were crude.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶1.2 Pre-AI strain · 3,500: one clause (Hanson et al.) -->
Before ChatGPT, output was already outgrowing the capacity to read and review it. Indexed articles were about 47% more numerous in 2022 than in 2016, outpacing the growth in practising scientists (Hanson et al. 2024). Peer review took more than 100 million reviewer-hours in 2020 (Aczel et al. 2021), and an Australian grant proposal took 34 working days to prepare, for a 21% chance of funding (Herbert et al. 2013). In large fields, a 'deluge of papers' ossifies the canon (Chu & Evans 2021). Growth is old, about 4% a year (Bornmann et al. 2021), and reviewer fatigue may be overstated (Breuning et al. 2015). But the gates were already the binding constraint, so anything that makes production cheap hits them first.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶1.3 The gates worked because outputs were costly (pivot paragraph) -->
Why did the gates work at all? Because what they counted was costly to produce, and the cost carried information. Three scarcities stood behind the outputs. Writing effort stood behind papers and grant applications; at 34 working days a proposal, effort alone limited how many one person could send. An expert's time stood behind each review, and years in a literature behind a credible synthesis. 'Signals are often informative insofar as they require differential knowledge and effort to produce' (Cowgill et al. 2026). Paper-based evaluation 'took hold because a paper took effort to produce, and that effort served as evidence' (Blekhman 2026), and before AI there was 'an easily discernible, practical limit to the rate at which independent submissions could be produced' (arXiv 2026). I do not defend the proxies, which were poor guides to individual merit long before AI (Seglen 1997; Brembs et al. 2013). The system relied on them, and they are failing faster than replacements appear (Figure 1). Nor is this the scarcity Hansen (2026) attacks. His is symbolic, the prestige that rejection produces, which he rightly calls 'not epistemically necessary'. Mine is a scarcity of costly inputs, which did epistemic work, however crudely.

<!-- CUT-3800 --><!-- ¶1.4 Park et al. only with its critics -->
I leave aside the reported decline in disruptive papers (Park et al. 2023), whose measure is contested: citation inflation biases it (Petersen et al. 2024), dataset artefacts 'can partially drive' the decline (Holst et al. 2026; but see Park et al. 2026), and truncation explains '88 percent' of it for patents (Macher et al. 2024). Nothing here depends on it.

## 2. AI makes counted output almost free

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.1 The marginal cost fell; use is broad and mostly ordinary · short versions: drop the last two sentences -->
Generative AI has cut the marginal cost of every counted output, and the use behind the cut is broad and mostly ordinary. At least 13.5% of 2024 PubMed abstracts show signs of LLM processing, up to about 40% in some subgroups (Kobak et al. 2025), as do up to 22% of sentences in computer-science abstracts (Liang et al. 2025) and 6.5–16.9% of review text at four AI conferences (Liang et al. 2024). These estimates come from detectors that misfire on non-native English writers (Liang et al. 2023), and most of what they detect is polishing. That is the point: the mass case is legitimate use, not misconduct. Readers who know Kobak's marker words may search this essay for them. Finding none would prove nothing, which is the problem in miniature.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.2 Signal 1, writing: honest use breaks it (core of contribution 1) -->
Take writing. Once anyone can produce fluent prose, prose stops indicating the quality of the research behind it. In 2.1 million preprints, LLM adopters raised their output by 23.7–89.3%, and LLM use 'reversed the relationship between writing complexity and paper quality' (Kusumegi et al. 2025) [RECHECK]. The output estimate is disputed, because dating adoption from an author's first flagged paper ties it to output (Renault et al. 2026, preprint) [RECHECK], but cleaner designs outside academia agree on the signal. Employers on a freelancing platform stopped valuing customised applications once LLMs arrived (Galdin & Silbert 2025, preprint) [RECHECK], and giving senders ChatGPT lowered screening accuracy by 4–9% (Cowgill et al. 2026). Journal editors find that simple prompting 'undermines writing quality as a screening signal' (Hanneke et al. 2026). Part of the loss is a gain. Polished English partly signalled a native speaker, at a cost that non-native scientists paid in time and effort (Amano et al. 2023), and adopters' gains appear larger for them (Kusumegi et al. 2025) [RECHECK]. So the problem is not misuse: the system is losing a partly unfair signal and has nothing to replace it. [AUTHOR: if true, one sentence saying that you, as a non-native writer, benefit from this yourself.]

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.3 Signal 2, reviewer time · short versions: drop the vendor estimate -->
Reviews raise the stakes, because they decide outcomes. A review used to certify that an expert had spent time on a paper. At ICLR 2024 at least 15.8% of reviews were AI-assisted, and borderline papers that received one were 4.9 percentage points more likely to be accepted (Latona et al. 2024, preprint) [RECHECK]. A vendor's contested detector flagged about one in five ICLR 2026 reviews as fully AI-written (Naddaf 2025) [RECHECK], and another puts the 2025 share at about 20% for ICLR and 12% for *Nature Communications* (Shen & Wang 2026, preprint) [RECHECK]. AI can also improve reviews: in a trial covering more than 20,000 ICLR 2025 reviews, 27% of reviewers given an AI agent's feedback updated their reviews (Thakkar et al. 2025, preprint) [RECHECK]. I grant this. A review simply no longer certifies an expert's time, and cheap reviews now meet cheap papers. [AUTHOR: optionally, one general sentence on a review you received and suspected was AI-written.]

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.4 Signal 1 again, in grants: the funder's answer was a cap -->
Grant applications used to be rationed by the weeks they took to write. In July 2025 the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) reported that 'the use of AI tools has enabled Principal Investigators to submit more than 40 distinct applications in a single application submission round'. It now accepts at most six applications per investigator a year and no longer treats applications 'substantially developed by AI' as original (NIH 2025); Parrilla (2023) had already argued that 'ChatGPT use shows that the grant-application system is broken'. Forty applications in a round looks like abuse, but the cap binds every investigator. And the originality rule rests on detectors, which misfire on non-native writers (Liang et al. 2023) and drift as authors drop the words that give them away (Geng & Trotta 2025, preprint) [RECHECK].

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.5 Signal 3, mastery time: the scarcity nobody counted (contribution 1b; novelty hedged) -->
The third scarcity was never counted, yet gatekeepers leaned on it. A credible synthesis, whether a review article or a background section that got the history right, showed years spent in a literature. AI collapses that time. AI tools reduce 'the informational cost of pivoting' (Huang et al. 2026) [RECHECK], and retrieval-grounded systems now cite about as accurately as experts on a benchmark (Asai et al. 2026). The cheapening is visible: in October 2025 arXiv's computer-science section stopped accepting unrefereed review and position papers, citing 'hundreds of review articles every month' (arXiv 2025). 'The apprenticeship pathway to scholarly identity is being severed' (Manabu 2026), and institutions 'can no longer reliably credit expertise based solely on authors' names' (Payan et al. 2026, preprint) [RECHECK]. The objection is that summarising is not mastery: 'Writing a plausible manuscript is now largely rule-based …; judging one is not' (Fourie 2026, preprint) [RECHECK]. I agree. Judgement is still scarce, but it is now invisible in the text that used to carry it, so the change that makes the ideal of Section 3 possible also removes a signal. [AUTHOR: an example from your own field, e.g. a background section for an unfamiliar area drafted in an evening.]

<!-- CUT-3500 --><!-- ¶2.6 Fraud amplifies but is not the core · 3,800: one sentence -->
Fraud amplifies all this, but it is not the core, and it predates LLMs: suspected fraudulent output was doubling about every 1.5 years, against about 15 for the literature (Richardson et al. 2025). AI-ready datasets now feed formulaic papers, with single-factor analyses of one US health survey rising from about four a year to 190 in 2024 (Suchak et al. 2025). Better detection would address this amplifier and leave untouched the cheap, legitimate output this essay is about.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.6b The tail and the distribution (new; answers outline §11, weakness 1) · 3,500: two sentences, merged into ¶2.4 -->
One objection runs through the institutional evidence. The caps were triggered by heavy submitters: NIH's investigators with more than 40 applications and, in arXiv's words, 'a relatively small proportion of authors are submitting a large number of low-quality papers' (arXiv 2026). That looks like a problem of the tail. My answer is that when an output gets cheaper for everyone, the whole distribution of output per person shifts, and the tail is where the shift first becomes visible, because that is where it first crosses a gatekeeper's threshold. Use is broad (Kobak et al. 2025), adopters produce more (Kusumegi et al. 2025) [RECHECK], and the total has moved: arXiv received 40,363 submissions in September 2026, against 20,569 two years earlier (arXiv 2026). The argument has a limit. Evidence that typical researchers produce more rests on detectors and a disputed output estimate, and no one has shown that ordinary use alone would overload a gate. If the tail carried the whole load, targeted sanctions would be the better tool, and blanket caps would be wrong for another reason.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.7 Legitimacy is irrelevant to the mechanism; accountability does not restore the signal -->
With all three scarcities gone, the system cannot read merit from what it counts, whoever answers for the output. Gartenberg et al. (2026) see a push toward 'more rather than better' research and rightly decline to say what level of AI use is appropriate; my point is that no level of legitimate use restores the signal. Nor does accountability, whose preservation Braun (2026) calls 'the deeper challenge'. Accountability says who answers for a paper and nothing about the effort or judgement in it, and disclosure 'alone rarely clarifies how AI-assisted work was performed' (Cwik 2026). arXiv allows AI use 'as long as it is disclosed', and its cap binds the disclosed and the undisclosed alike (arXiv 2026). This essay is the case in point: disclosed, accountable and still unreadable as evidence of effort.

## 3. The ideal: what AI could do for science

<!-- CUT-3500 --><!-- ¶3.1 Label and problem: the burden of knowledge -->
This section changes register. It describes what AI could do for science, and it is normative: it says what I would want, and it is the part of this essay I would most like to be right about. Its one discipline is that each ideal rests on a mechanism that already works somewhere. The problem is old. As knowledge grows, researchers need longer training and cover narrower slices of it, so discovery moves into teams (Jones 2009; Wuchty et al. 2007), and in crowded fields new ideas struggle to break through (Chu & Evans 2021). The fall in cost that breaks the signals of Section 2 could lift part of this burden of knowledge, letting researchers stay literate in neighbouring fields without losing depth in their own.

<!-- CUT-3500 --><!-- ¶3.2 Ideal 1: undiscovered public knowledge -->
The oldest mechanism is forty years old. In 1986 Don Swanson joined two literatures that did not cite each other and found a hypothesis that neither contained, that fish oil might help patients with Raynaud's syndrome; he called such latent links 'undiscovered public knowledge' (Swanson 1986). Literature-based discovery stayed marginal as adoption and validation lagged (Smalheiser & Torvik 2008), and checking candidate links took expert reading time. That reading is what language models make cheap. Imagine every field with a patient reader who knows what the next field over has already found.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶3.3 Ideal 2: synthesis with correct citations, in the open · 3,500: one sentence -->
The second is synthesis that can be checked. General-purpose chatbots invent references (Walters & Wilder 2023), but systems built for science and grounded in retrieval do far better. OpenScholar, an open model that answers from about 45 million open-access papers, matched human experts in citation accuracy on its benchmark while a leading proprietary model fabricated citations in 78–90% of cases, although its authors say that full automation of synthesis is not yet possible (Asai et al. 2026). Similar claims are made for an agent called PaperQA2 (Skarlinski et al. 2024, preprint) [RECHECK]. A benchmark is not a field, so this shows only that the mechanism exists. But a synthesis that cites its sources correctly can be checked, and checking is what science needs.

<!-- CUT-3500 --><!-- ¶3.4 Ideal 3: AI aimed at the gaps between communities -->
The third aims AI at what communities are unlikely to find by themselves. Models that account for how expertise is distributed predict future discoveries better and, steered away from what existing communities can reach, propose promising 'alien' hypotheses (Sourati & Evans 2023) [RECHECK]; machine learning can also generate 'novel, interpretable hypotheses' from data (Ludwig & Mullainathan 2024). The group that built AI to look for blind spots also finds that AI, as scientists use it, contracts the focus of science (Hao et al. 2026). Design matters, and adoption follows incentives.

<!-- CUT-3500 --><!-- ¶3.5 Ideal 4: cheap pivots; the barrier is institutional -->
The fourth is the cheap pivot. Huang et al. (2026) [RECHECK] picture an ecologist moving into genomics who 'can now traverse unfamiliar literature faster', and after 2022 more scientists did publish in distant fields and enter new ones (Zheng et al. 2026, preprint) [RECHECK]. The barriers are institutional. The more interdisciplinary an Australian Research Council proposal, the lower its chance of funding (Bromham et al. 2016), and the impact of new work 'steeply declines the further a researcher moves from their previous work' (Hill et al. 2025). The technology lowers the price of moving; evaluation still charges it. [AUTHOR: one cross-field connection from your own field that you would want AI to surface.]

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶3.6 Precondition: open, efficient, auditable tools -->
All of this holds only for tools that science can inspect, reproduce and run itself. A proprietary model can change silently between two analyses, which is why using one in research should need explicit justification (Palmer et al. 2024) and why open models are the ethical way forward (Spirling 2023). Fully open models exist: OLMo releases its weights, training data, code and logs (Groeneveld et al. 2024), and OpenScholar is open. Openness is necessary, but it is not sufficient. A shared model can lower collective accuracy even when it is individually better, whoever owns it (Kleinberg & Raghavan 2021), and 'open' AI still depends on compute, data and frameworks that a few firms control (Widder et al. 2024).

## 4. What AI is actually doing

### 4a. The evidence

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4a.1 The largest study: individual gains, collective contraction, for AI as a method, compared cross-sectionally -->
What is AI doing to the range of science? The largest study finds individual gains and collective contraction, for a narrower kind of AI, and with a narrower design, than its headlines suggest. Of 41.3 million papers in six natural sciences, Hao et al. (2026) classed 310,957 (0.75%) as using AI as a research method [RECHECK]. Scientists who use AI this way publish 3.02 times more papers, receive 4.84 times more citations and become project leaders 1.37 years earlier, while AI papers occupy a 4.63% smaller 'knowledge extent' and work building on them is 22% less interconnected by citation. Three cautions apply. The AI is mostly machine learning, largely before ChatGPT, and the generative-era analyses are 'preliminary', with causality not identified [RECHECK]. The contraction compares papers, not scientists over time. And the 22% is citation density, not contact between people. I read the study as the strongest evidence that AI concentrates where data are rich, or, as the accompanying News & Views put it, that it 'automates established fields' (Storey 2026).

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4a.2 Generative-era ideation converges · 3,800: add one clause from ¶4a.3 · 3,500: one sentence (Bao et al.) -->
Studies of AI's own ideas point the same way, though all are preprints and none observes what scientists choose. LLM research ideas were rated more novel than experts' (5.64 against 4.84 out of 10), yet of 4,000 generated per topic only about 200 were not duplicates (Si et al. 2024, preprint) [RECHECK], and after experts executed them they lost significantly more of their scores than human ideas did (Si et al. 2025, preprint) [RECHECK]. Non-reasoning models 'collapse into a narrow "hivemind"', 'no model spontaneously proposes null hypotheses', and after ChatGPT's release 'null claims are sharply suppressed and ideas contract' across 39 million papers (Bao et al. 2026, preprint) [RECHECK]. LLM hypotheses also score 15–23% lower on a measure of new direction (Liu et al. 2026, preprint) [RECHECK].

<!-- CUT-3800 --><!-- ¶4a.3 Homogenisation experiments · 3,800: one clause inside ¶4a.2 -->
Experiments outside science show the same pairing: AI-assisted stories were more creative but 'more similar to each other' (Doshi & Hauser 2024), and LLM help made essays, ideas and academic prose more alike (Padmakumar & He 2024; Anderson et al. 2024; Meincke et al. 2025 [RECHECK]; Lin & Zhu 2025, preprint [RECHECK]). But in a data-science contest AI homogenised how solutions were expressed and left the strategies alone (Burtch 2026, preprint) [RECHECK], which is why Box 2 relies on references rather than text.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4a.4 The contrary evidence, stated plainly: individuals explore more (answers outline §11, weakness 2) -->
The contrary evidence deserves a full hearing, because it is the best we have on what scientists themselves do. Zheng et al. (2026, preprint) [RECHECK] followed 775,323 scientists. After 2022 they 'increasingly published across more intellectually distant fields and entered fields in which they had not previously worked', most of all established scientists and authors from non-English-speaking low- and middle-income countries; heavier users of AI writing were already more exploratory before LLMs. Adopters 'access and cite more diverse prior work' (Kusumegi et al. 2025), and in a matched panel of social scientists the gains were largest for early-career and non-English-speaking authors (Filimonovic et al. 2025, preprint) [RECHECK]. Taken at face value, this refutes any claim that scientists who use AI stop exploring. It does not settle what happens to science as a whole. If many individuals move into the same AI-amenable areas, each moves further from their own past work while the diversity of the system falls. That reconciliation is my reading, not a finding: Zheng et al. do not report where the movers land.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4a.5 What the evidence supports, at system level -->
So the evidence supports less than the headlines. Generative AI raises output, AI as a method concentrates where data are rich, LLM ideation converges, and individual exploration may be rising; one synthesis finds the evidence on novelty 'ambiguous or negative' (Castellacci et al. 2026, preprint) [RECHECK]. Whether AI narrows science overall is not established, and Section 2 does not depend on it. I state narrowing as a second-order risk: a hypothesis about the system.

### 4b. The hypothesis

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4b.1 Metric incentives channel AI into exploitation; a hypothesis now shared -->
The hypothesis is that scientists use AI to do faster what they already know how to do, because count-based metrics with short time windows reward exactly that. [AUTHOR: two sentences, in your own voice, on where the hypothesis came from.] I reached it independently, but it is now shared. Huang et al. (2026) [RECHECK] state it almost word for word, 'AI makes extending a familiar research line faster and cheaper than entering unfamiliar terrain, and existing incentive systems reward such speed', and conclude that 'The problem is not technological, but institutional'; Kapoor & Narayanan (2025), Xizhe Zhang (2026) and Messeri & Crockett (2024) argue along similar lines. The mechanism is well founded. Processes that refine exploitation faster than exploration are 'likely to become effective in the short run but self-destructive in the long run' (March 1991). Novel papers are less cited in short windows (Wang et al. 2017), evaluators score novel proposals lower (Boudreau et al. 2016), the extra reward for innovation 'does not compensate for the risk of failing to publish' (Foster et al. 2015), and the pivot penalty is growing (Hill et al. 2025). Journal editors, by contrast, select for novelty (Teplitskiy et al. 2022); the bias sits in short-window metrics and grant panels (Packalen & Bhattacharya 2020), which is where AI-cheapened output is counted.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4b.2 The rival: technological affordance, plus selection -->
The rival explanation needs no incentives at all. AI works best on well-posed, data-rich problems, so it would yield exploitation even under neutral evaluation. In Hao et al.'s own analysis, data availability is the main driver of where AI concentrates, while topicality, original impact and funding are 'almost unrelated' [RECHECK]; and AI prediction costs have 'fallen roughly 100-fold in the past two years' (Huang et al. 2026) [RECHECK], which favours fields already rich in data. Outside science, when a coding assistant supported one programming language and not another, incremental contributions rose more than substantive ones (Yeverechyahu et al. 2024, preprint) [RECHECK], and consultants using AI did better inside its capability frontier but were 19% less likely to be correct outside it (Dell'Acqua et al. 2026) [RECHECK]. Selection is a third possibility, since heavy users were already different before LLMs (Zheng et al. 2026, preprint). Huang et al. blend the first two mechanisms, and the remedies of theirs that I have read, funding data infrastructure and ending pivot penalties, map onto them [RECHECK].

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4b.3 Why the difference matters -->
The difference decides which lever to pull. If incentives drive narrowing, changing what is counted would redirect AI towards exploration; if affordance does, data infrastructure for neglected problems would; if both act, funders need their relative weight. Box 2 sets out a discriminating test. I have found no study that runs it, and I plan to run it as a preregistered study [AUTHOR: confirm, or replace with an invitation to the field].

### 4c. Governance as rationing

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4c.1 Reform machinery exists, but it is voluntary and pre-AI (answers outline §11, weakness 3) · 3,500: one sentence -->
Reform that changes what is counted exists, and it deserves credit. DORA (2012) asked that journal metrics stop standing in for the quality of individual work, and CoARA (2022) commits its 600 to more than 830 signatories, depending on the source [RECHECK], to qualitative assessment supported by responsible metrics. Where adopted, reforms can work: Registered Reports report 44% positive results against 96% in the standard psychology literature (Scheel et al. 2021), and Utrecht University dropped the impact factor from hiring and promotion (Woolston 2021). But the machinery is voluntary and uneven. Institutions signed DORA without changing practice (Curry 2018), and a 2026 study for the European Commission found engagement 'broad and increasing' and progress 'uneven' (European Commission 2026). None of it was designed for machine-scale output.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4c.2 The binding responses are caps: rationing that restores scarcity by decree (Table 1; short versions: a compact list) -->
The binding responses to AI-era volume have been caps (Table 1). NIH allows six applications per investigator a year. arXiv's computer-science section stopped accepting unrefereed reviews and position papers in October 2025 (arXiv 2025); in May 2026, according to a press report, arXiv began banning for a year authors who leave LLM output unchecked (TechCrunch 2026) [RECHECK]; and since 1 October 2026 each submitter may post two papers a month, a 'stopgap' (arXiv 2026). SocArXiv generally takes one paper per author per week (SocArXiv 2026). ICLR 2027 allows 20 submissions per author but only one for authors without an acceptance at a major AI conference, unless a co-author has one, and it expects more than 62,000 submissions, against 490 a decade ago; AISTATS 2027, ACL and TMLR have quotas too (Singh Chawla 2026a) [RECHECK]. Triage is necessary, with some 300 volunteer moderators checking about 1,000 manuscripts a day at arXiv (Singh Chawla 2026b) [RECHECK]. Yet a cap restores only the scarcity of submissions. The information that scarcity carried does not return, and what is counted stays the same. Two conference caps even allocate slots by prior counted success, a Matthew effect written into the rules (Merton 1968) that critics call a 'double standard' (Yener, quoted in Singh Chawla 2026a).

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4c.3 Rationing versus pricing versus redesign; Hansen as the foil -->
Rationing is one of three answers to cheap output; the others are pricing and redesign. Fourie (2026, preprint) [RECHECK] observes that review 'is therefore rationed, not priced', and that 'A journal that keeps its stamp scarce by rejecting what it cannot read is not thereby protecting the stamp's value'. In one model, only 3% of the optimal fee covers direct review costs, and 'the remaining 97% reflects congestion screening and prestige stabilization' (Lopez-Lira & Seyfi 2026, working paper) [RECHECK]; others propose fees contingent on AI use with paid reviewers, refundable deposits or fairer quotas (Atasu 2026, working paper; Hanneke et al. 2026; Shah 2026, preprint [RECHECK]). Hansen (2026) goes the other way: scarcity is the disease, so publish all sound work, with 'Prestige curation without scarcity'. The reply is to separate two scarcities. His is symbolic, produced by rejection; the one AI removed is a scarcity of costly inputs. Abolishing the first still leaves cheap output to be evaluated, and Hansen himself names what remains, 'the scarcity of interpretive attention'. Pricing has its own cost, which I infer but no study shows: fees weigh most on researchers with the least money.

<!-- CUT-3800 --><!-- ¶4c.4 Precedent: China changed what counts by decree, with weak effects · 3,800: one clause survives in ¶4c.5 -->
China shows that a state can change what counts by decree, and what follows. In 2020 its ministries abolished rewards based directly on SCI indicators and their use as a direct basis for hiring or degrees (MOE & MOST 2020), introducing 'representative works' instead [RECHECK] [CITATION NEEDED: MOST & MOF 2020, Guokefajian [2020] No. 37, in research note G2 Q6, search summary only]; in 2026 the Chinese Academy of Sciences ended its journal ranking table (Xu 2026); and the 2025 'AI+' plan asks universities to credit open-source contributions (State Council 2025), a change meant to promote AI, not to cope with its output. Effects were weak. Early documents read as 'a communication exercise to appease public anger' (Shu et al. 2022) [RECHECK], and discretion moved to administrators and senior scholars, at the expense of early-career researchers ([authors to fill] 2025) [RECHECK]. The lesson concerns mechanism, not regime: without capacity for qualitative judgement, discretion migrates to gatekeepers, and AI-era volume consumes exactly that capacity.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4c.5 None of the responses changes what is counted -->
Together, the responses sort into three kinds. Disclosure rules govern conduct: no AI authors, declared use (Thorp 2023; Nature 2023), guidance at 87% of the top 100 journals by October 2023 (Ganjavi et al. 2024), no generative AI in NIH review (NIH 2023). Caps ration volume. Reform of what counts stays voluntary and predates AI. None changes what is counted in response to cheap output, so the default is a fallback. Blekhman (2026) predicts a fall back 'on older, cruder proxies, such as journal prestige and impact factor, which would undo the past decade of research assessment reform'. Rationing is a second fallback, already under way. Change is 'difficult, but not impossible' (Hatch & Curry 2020), but caps keyed to past acceptances lock in incumbents, so the window to change what is counted is now.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶5.6 Two drivers of narrowing (contribution 4) -->
Narrowing therefore has two separable drivers. The internal one is the subject of Section 4: academic incentives decide what AI is used for. The external one is concentration. A few firms decide what the tools are, what they are trained on and what they learn from users; shared models make outputs alike whatever the incentives (Kleinberg & Raghavan 2021; Bommasani et al. 2022); and proprietary tools change silently under their users (Palmer et al. 2024). Each driver has been argued separately, the internal one by Huang et al. (2026) [RECHECK], the external one by Chesterman & Loy (2026, preprint) [RECHECK], Manning (2026) and Lindebaum et al. (2025). Separated, they make different predictions: narrowing that varies with the intensity of incentives (Box 2), or convergence by model family, which is now identifiable paper by paper (Dunivin 2026, preprint) [RECHECK]. They are entangled, since firms build for the market that academia creates, but separable in analysis and partly in data. No study yet links chat-data harvesting to the homogenisation of science, and I claim no such link.

Open is not independent (Widder et al. 2024), and Chinese weights carry state-shaped content. But the tasks of Section 3, checked synthesis and the search for gaps, do not need the frontier. ## Conclusion

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶6.1 Beyond rationing: change what is counted, not how much is submitted (proposals, not solutions; answers outline §11, weakness 3) -->
What would be needed beyond rationing? I can offer proposals, not solutions: none has been tested against AI-era volume, each has costs, and most are borrowed. One is to count less and read more, capping what evaluators consider rather than what authors submit (Frith 2020), as China's 'representative works' attempted. Another is to price review instead of rationing it, through fees, deposits or paid reviewers (Fourie 2026, preprint; Lopez-Lira & Seyfi 2026, working paper; Hansen 2026), at some cost in equity. A third is to seek costly signals that AI cannot fake cheaply, such as proctored tests of an author's understanding (Payan et al. 2026, preprint) [RECHECK], or documentation of how the work was done in place of bare disclosure (Cwik 2026). A fourth is to reward exploration directly, through long-horizon funding of the kind that moved Howard Hughes Medical Institute investigators towards 'novel lines of inquiry' (Azoulay et al. 2011), choosing between Huang et al.'s levers with the test in Box 2. The fifth is public, open AI infrastructure for science. The weaknesses are known: the case for narrative CVs rests on 28 opinion pieces and 7 experiments (Bordignon et al. 2023), and more peer review brings its own costs and biases (Abramo 2024). That argues for deliberate experiment, run and measured by funders and institutions (Nielsen & Qiu 2022), instead of a drift into rationing by default. ## Box 2 | How this hypothesis could be tested

<!-- KEEP-3800 -->
The hypothesis is that metric incentives channel generative AI into exploitation: scientists use it to do faster what they already know how to do. Others now state it (Huang et al. 2026) [RECHECK]; I have found no test of it. Hao et al. (2026) cannot settle it. Their 'AI' is mostly machine learning used as a method, their contraction compares papers rather than following scientists, and in their data, data availability rather than incentives best predicts where AI concentrates [RECHECK].

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- short versions: drop one regime -->
A discriminating test is a triple difference. Within scientists, compare exploration before and after November 2022 by intensity of AI use, and ask whether the effect is larger where counting is harsher: before versus after a first last-author paper; under evaluation regimes that count publications versus those that do not; and under short grant cycles versus long-horizon funding of the kind that encourages exploration (Azoulay et al. 2011). If incentives drive narrowing, AI users should narrow more where counting is harsher; if AI simply goes where the data are, the effect should track data availability instead. Because incentive intensity can itself correlate with how data-rich a field is, comparisons must be made within fields and topics, with data availability measured and interacted rather than assumed away.

<!-- CUT-3500 --><!-- measurement paragraph; the outline drops it in the short versions -->
Exploration should be measured three ways, per paper and per author-year: distance from a scientist's own earlier work, novelty against the field, and the diversity of the whole system. Reference-based measures come first, because AI polishing makes abstracts alike and so mimics narrowing. Adoption must be measured independently of output (Renault et al. 2026, preprint) [RECHECK], detectors misclassify non-native writers (Liang et al. 2023), and arXiv's 2025–26 rules change who appears in the data.

<!-- CUT-3800 --><!-- optional extension, master only (outline, notes on Box 2) -->
The external driver of Section 5 adds a second prediction, convergence by model family, testable once each paper's model family is identified (Dunivin 2026, preprint) [RECHECK].

<!-- KEEP-3800 -->
The hypothesis is falsifiable. It is weakened if AI users move further from their earlier work than comparable non-users, as early evidence suggests (Zheng et al. 2026, preprint [RECHECK]; Kusumegi et al. 2025), or if the effect does not vary with incentive intensity. The data are open (OpenAlex n.d. [RECHECK]; Semantic Scholar n.d.).
