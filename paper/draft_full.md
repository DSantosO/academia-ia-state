# The cost was the signal: why academic evaluation is not ready for AI used well

**Academic evaluation has long inferred merit partly from what its outputs cost to produce: the weeks behind a grant application, an expert's hours behind a review, the years of reading behind a synthesis. Generative AI lowers those costs even when it is used openly and well, so the outputs reveal less about the work behind them, and the damage does not depend on misuse. Institutions have answered mainly with caps on submissions, from NIH, arXiv and AI conferences, which restore the scarcity of submissions but not the information it carried. I argue that evaluation must change what it counts and how it reads, propose a test of whether incentives or the pull of data-rich problems would drive any narrowing of AI-era science, and document how this essay was itself made with AI agents.**

My claim is that academic evaluation is not prepared for a world in which AI is used generally. It infers merit partly from outputs that used to be costly to produce (Figure 1), and generative AI makes the written part of them cheap even when it is used well. Much of this is shared ground. Early commentators expected AI to accelerate overproduction, expose a failing system and show the grant system to be broken (Carrigan 2023; Kingsley 2023; Parrilla 2023). Since then it has been argued that cheap output completes, and so destroys, publish-or-perish (Manabu 2026), that polish no longer indicates merit (Hansen 2026), that AI-generated papers break the rough link between effort and value (Nature 2026) and that paper counts have become meaningless (Blekhman 2026).

What I add is narrower, and each part has a close precedent. First, rationing: Blekhman (2026) predicts a fallback to journal prestige, but a different fallback is already under way. ## 1. How the system works

<!-- CUT-3500 --><!-- ¶1.1 Counts stand in for hard-to-see qualities · Butler 2003 cut (memory-sourced; the audit could not verify it, R04); Quan et al. worded as in the abstract (O-08) · compressed (16 Oct) -->
Evaluation judges qualities that are hard to see, such as originality and rigour, by counting what is easy to see: publication counts, impact factors and grant income dominate the criteria for hiring and promotion (Moher et al. 2018), and researchers respond. Italy's citation thresholds for promotion were followed by a sharp rise in self-citation (Seeber et al. 2019), and Chinese universities offered cash rewards of US$30 to US$165,000 for a paper in a journal indexed by Web of Science (Quan et al. 2017). Yet Fanelli (2018) calls the narrative of a science in crisis mistaken, and stable Polish incentives raised output with no measured fall in quality (Korytkowski & Kulczycki 2019): the system was strained before AI, and gameable where its incentives were crude.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶1.2 Pre-AI strain · Park et al. and its critics dropped for length (brief §6: it "can be included"; nothing depends on it) · 3,800: one clause (Hanson et al.) · Bornmann et al. dropped (16 Oct; length) -->
Before ChatGPT, output was already outgrowing the capacity to read and review it. Indexed articles were about 47% more numerous in 2022 than in 2016 (Hanson et al. 2024), peer review took more than 100 million reviewer-hours in 2020 (Aczel et al. 2021), and an Australian grant proposal took 34 working days to prepare, for a 21% chance of funding (Herbert et al. 2013). But the gates were already the binding constraint, so anything that makes production cheap reaches them first.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶1.3 The gates worked because outputs were costly (pivot paragraph) · differential cost (R1-2b), panels as deliberation (R2-8), Adeyeye (R1-1); the distinction from Hansen stated only here (R2-7d) -->
Why did the gates work at all? Because what they counted was costly to produce, and the cost carried information. Three costs stood behind the outputs: the effort of writing papers and grant applications, an expert's time behind each review, and years in a literature behind a credible synthesis. I will call them the three scarcities. They are not Hansen's (2026) symbolic scarcity, the prestige that rejection produces, which he rightly calls unnecessary for knowledge; they lay in costly inputs, and however crudely, they did epistemic work. What carried information was less the cost than its unevenness: "signals are often informative insofar as they require differential knowledge and effort to produce" (Cowgill et al. 2026). Blekhman (2026) traces paper-based evaluation to the same fact, and Adeyeye (2026) [RECHECK: SSRN abstract only; not audited] applies signalling theory to the publication itself. Panels do more than read signals; they deliberate and negotiate what excellence means [CITATION NEEDED: Lamont 2009, *How Professors Think*; suggested by the internal review, not in the notes]. But deliberation needs a manageable docket, and cost kept it manageable. I do not defend the proxies themselves, which were poor guides to individual merit long before AI (Seglen 1997; Brembs et al. 2013).

## 2. AI makes what the system counts cheap to write

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.1 Which layer is cheap (E3); use is broad; detectors measure style, not purpose (R1-4d) · the marker-word joke cut for length · Suchak et al. moved to ¶2.9 -->
Generative AI has cut the cost of the written layer of every counted output. That layer is the whole of a grant application, a review or a review article, but only part of an experimental paper, whose cost lies mostly in the experiments. Use is broad: at least 13.5% of 2024 PubMed abstracts show signs of processing by large language models (LLMs), up to about 40% in some subgroups (Kobak et al. 2025). These estimates rest on detectors, which misfire on non-native English writers (Liang et al. 2023) and measure style rather than purpose. They show that use is common, and much of it is permitted: Springer Nature classes polishing language with an LLM as permitted, low-risk use (Nature Portfolio n.d.).

Take writing first. Once fluent prose is cheap for everyone, it says less about the research behind it. In a study of entrepreneurship and hiring, giving senders ChatGPT lowered the accuracy with which they were screened by 4–9% (Cowgill et al. 2026), and a study of 2.1 million preprints found that LLM use "reversed the relationship between writing complexity and paper quality" (Kusumegi et al. 2025). Part of the loss is a gain. Polished English partly signalled a native speaker, at a cost that non-native scientists paid in time and effort (Amano et al. 2023), and in Cowgill et al.'s study access to ChatGPT made screening more accurate, not less, for senders from non-English-speaking countries. The system is losing a partly unfair signal and has nothing yet to put in its place. [AUTHOR: if true, one sentence saying that you, as a non-native writer, benefit from this yourself.]

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.3 Signal 2, reviewer time · machine-learning venues named as such (R1-7); Thakkar et al. "updated", not "improved" (R1 minor 5); Latona et al. now published as Russo et al. Reviews matter more, because they decide outcomes. Most of the evidence comes from machine-learning conferences, whose reviews are public. At ICLR 2024 at least 15.8% of reviews were AI-assisted, and borderline papers that received one were 4.9 percentage points more likely to be accepted (Russo et al. 2025); in 2025 a detector classed about 20% of ICLR reviews and 12% of *Nature Communications* reviews as AI-generated (Shen & Wang 2026). Whether or not the use is disclosed, a review no longer shows that an expert spent time on the paper.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.4 Signal 1 again, in grants · NIH's own qualifier added (audit O-16); "binds" corrected to "applies" (R1-3a); no longer implies that NIH uses detectors (R1 minor 6) -->
Grant applications used to be rationed by the weeks they took to write. In July 2025 the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) reported that "the use of AI tools has enabled Principal Investigators to submit more than 40 distinct applications in a single application submission round". It now accepts at most six applications per investigator a year and no longer treats applications "substantially developed by AI" as original (NIH 2025). NIH expects the cap to affect "a relatively small number of Principal Investigators", but it applies to every investigator, whatever their conduct, which makes it rationing rather than a sanction. NIH says it uses detection technology to identify AI-generated applications, but detectors drift as authors drop the words that give them away (Geng & Trotta 2025).

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.5 Signal 3, mastery time: the scarcity nobody counted · labelled conceptual (R1-7); arXiv's rule shows that synthesis-shaped texts got cheap, not mastery (R1 minor 7) -->
The third scarcity was never counted, yet gatekeepers leaned on it. A credible synthesis, whether a review article or a background section that got the history right, showed years spent in a literature. The claim is conceptual: I know of no study of how far evaluators relied on it. AI shortens that time, lowering what Huang et al. (2026) call the informational cost of pivoting. Texts shaped like syntheses have certainly become cheap: in October 2025 arXiv's computer-science section, faced with hundreds of review articles a month, stopped accepting reviews and position papers that had not passed peer review (arXiv 2025a). Summarising is not mastery, and judgement remains scarce: "Writing a plausible manuscript is now largely rule-based …; judging one is not" (Fourie 2026). But judgement no longer shows in the text that used to display it. <!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.7 The information mechanism (R1-2: split from congestion; "invisible", not "gone") · Blekhman's hollow papers vs real research (author's decision) · the closing sentence on this essay dropped (16 Oct; it repeated ¶0.1 and ¶C.1) -->
With all three costs lowered, what the system counts reveals less about the work behind it, whoever answers for the output. The signals have not disappeared: judgement and mastery remain scarce, and some writers still write better than others. But AI narrows the differences in cost on which the signals relied, apparently most for less fluent writers, so a fluent paper, a competent review or a well-cited synthesis separates less clearly those who did the work from those who did less of it. Blekhman's (2026) case is the plausible paper produced without any research. Mine is the paper that reports real research and still loses its signal, because the effort behind it has become invisible. No rule about appropriate use, a question Gartenberg et al. (2026) decline to settle, restores that signal. Nor does accountability, whose preservation Braun (2026) calls the deeper challenge: it says who answers for a paper, not what effort or judgement went into it.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.8 (was ¶2.6b) The congestion mechanism, shown on the author's key figure: the mathematics surge (confirmed at commit 41f795d) answers the review's tail objection (R1-2, R1-4e), since the heavy-producer share rose because hundreds of established authors crossed the threshold. The second mechanism is congestion: cheap output overloading gates of fixed capacity. In my companion analysis, new preprints in the four quarters to September 2026 exceeded 2022 levels in every field examined, by 44% in physics, 50% in biology, 59% in mathematics and 146% in computer science (Figure 3). Mathematics shows how the growth is distributed. On arXiv, the share of new mathematics papers with an author who posted five or more in the same quarter rose from 5.3% in January 2025 to 19.3% in September 2026 (Figure 4). Namesakes inflate the level, since arXiv records only names, and so may authors who post finished work in batches, but under stricter matching the share still at least doubled, and on most measures it roughly tripled. The rise began in March 2026, seven months before arXiv's cap, and came from established mathematicians scaling up across team sizes and subfields: 611 of the 693 prolific authors of the third quarter of 2026 had posted mathematics papers earlier in the period. That AI caused the rise is an inference, but the circumstantial evidence is strong: the timing, a parallel rise in papers that mention AI tools, the Lean proof assistant or Erdős, and an independent estimate that arXiv mathematics ran about 12% above its counterfactual trend in January–August 2026 (Nee et al. 2026). So the heavy tail that the caps target is not a handful of outliers: hundreds of ordinary researchers crossed the threshold, and the count no longer measures what it did.

Biology differs, but only recently. Since 2022 it has grown no less than physics; the fields part in 2026. Year on year, preprints grew by 78% in mathematics, 41% in computer science and 26% in physics, while bioRxiv has grown steadily, by 10–13% a year since 2023, with no break in 2026 (bioRxiv 2026), although biologists write with LLMs about as much as physicists (Lin & Zhu 2025; Holzwarth et al. 2026). The difference lies in what AI can produce, not in who uses it. AI cheapens the steps that need only text, code or existing data and can be checked cheaply; experiments cost what they did, as Blekhman (2026) argues. Within fields, too (Figure 5), theoretical physics grew by 20% in the first nine months of 2026, far outside its range since 2017, while experiment stayed within its own; computational biology has outgrown wet-lab biology only since 2025, mostly in bioinformatics, much of which is itself AI research. Where no new experiment is needed, biomedical output has surged: publications based on nine heavily mined open health datasets tripled between 2022 and 2025 (Spick et al. 2026). ### Misuse is the visible tip

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.9 New in draft 2 (author's decision, 16 Oct 2026; replaces ¶2.6, fraud as amplifier). Misuse is well researched: one clause each, not a review. · 3,800: first two and last sentences · Suchak et al.'s 190 counts only to 9 October 2024 (note 8); Maupin et al. 2025 (redundant NHANES papers 17-fold) left out for length -->
Misuse is real and well documented. Paper mills were outgrowing the literature long before ChatGPT (Richardson et al. 2025); hallucinated references and leftover chatbot text prompted arXiv's one-year bans (Section 4c); and authors have hidden prompts in manuscripts to steer AI reviewers (Lin 2025) [RECHECK: arXiv abstract only; not audited]. Between misuse and legitimate use lies a grey zone of formulaic output. Single-factor association studies built on the US NHANES health survey rose from about four a year in 2014–21 to 190 in 2024 (to 9 October); many have weak designs, and the authors link the surge to AI-assisted, paper-mill-style production from AI-ready datasets (Suchak et al. 2025). The template needs no fraud, only a public dataset and a language model, and where the data come ready-made the whole paper becomes cheap.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶2.10 New in draft 2 (author's decision): why the distinction does not help gatekeepers; never implies that the prolific authors misuse AI; leads into Section 4c -->
The mathematics data show why the distinction does not help gatekeepers. Nothing in them suggests that the prolific authors misuse AI: most are established mathematicians, and their papers mention AI tools barely more often than everyone else's (1.9% against 1.6%, a lower bound from metadata alone). Yet no one reading the record could split the rise into misuse and legitimate high productivity, and detectors cannot settle it either. So institutions limit volume for everyone (Section 4c). Misuse is where the problem becomes visible, not where it ends. Remove every paper mill, fabricated reference and machine-written review, and the effort behind honest work is still invisible in the text.

## 3. The ideal: what AI could do for science

<!-- CUT-3500 --><!-- ¶3.1 Label and problem · Blekhman's five goals as the hook (author's decision) · "This section changes register" cut (R2-7e) -->
What follows is normative, and its one discipline is that each ideal rests on a mechanism that already works somewhere. Blekhman (2026), asking of biology what Tao (2026) asks of mathematics, supplies a yardstick: biology research is for explaining living systems, improving health, building resources such as data and methods, training the next generation and keeping the trust of the public that pays. In the world I would want, AI advances those goals, not the paper count. The obstacle is old: as knowledge grows, researchers need longer training and cover narrower slices of it (Jones 2009), and the cheapening that weakens the signals of Section 2 could lift part of this burden, letting researchers stay literate in neighbouring fields without losing depth in their own.

<!-- CUT-3500 --><!-- ¶3.2 Ideal 1: undiscovered public knowledge, now including the former ¶3.4 (Sourati & Evans), merged for length · evidence level stated (R2-4a) -->
The oldest mechanism is forty years old: in 1986 Don Swanson joined two literatures that did not cite each other and found that fish oil might help patients with Raynaud's syndrome (Swanson 1986). The method stayed marginal (Smalheiser & Torvik 2008), but models that account for how expertise is distributed can propose "alien" hypotheses that existing communities are unlikely to reach (Sourati & Evans 2023). Imagine every field with a patient reader who knows what the next field over has already found.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶3.3 Ideal 2: synthesis that can be checked · Asai et al. quoted exactly (audit O-04); the link to evaluation (R2-4b) and the illusion of understanding (R2-4c) added · the Asai et al. quotation is now paraphrased (16 Oct, to cut quotations) -->
The second is synthesis that can be checked. ### 4a. The evidence

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4a.1 The largest study: gains and contraction, for AI as a method · first sentence per R1 line note; "times as many" (R1-4a); the 22% defined as in the source and the caveats quoted (audit R21, R22) -->
The largest study of AI's effect on the range of science finds individual gains and collective contraction, but for AI used as a research method, and by comparing papers rather than following scientists. Of 41.3 million papers in six natural sciences, Hao et al. (2026) classed 310,957 (0.75%) as using AI this way. Their authors publish 3.02 times as many papers, receive 4.84 times as many citations and lead projects 1.37 years earlier, while AI papers cover a 4.63% narrower range of topics, and papers that cite the same AI paper cite one another 22% less. The AI is mostly machine learning, largely before ChatGPT; the generative-AI analysis is, in the authors' words, "necessarily more preliminary"; and they "cannot fully identify the causal linkage between AI adoption and scientific impact". The 22% measures citation density and says nothing about contact between people.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4a.2 Generative-era ideation converges · Si et al. now published (R23); Burtch quoted exactly (R29); one clause from the removed ¶4a.3 · 3,800: first two sentences -->
Studies of AI's own ideas point the same way, though none observes what scientists choose. Of 4,000 LLM research ideas generated per topic, only about 200 were not duplicates (Si et al. 2025), and non-reasoning models "collapse into a narrow 'hivemind'" (Bao et al. 2026). In experiments, AI assistance makes collective output more alike (Doshi & Hauser 2024), though in Kaggle contests code converged in syntax with "no evidence of homogenization in approach or intent" (Burtch 2026), which is why Box 2 measures exploration through references.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4a.4 The contrary evidence, stated plainly · "the best we have" and the staged "deserves a full hearing" removed (R2-7c,f); the reconciliation is now a prediction in Box 2 (R1-4f) · Kusumegi et al.'s "more diverse citing" dropped (author's decision after the audit): it comes from the same within-author event study, with adoption dated by first detection, that Renault et al. The best evidence on what scientists themselves do points the other way. Following 775,323 scientists, Zheng et al. So the evidence supports less than the headlines. AI as a method concentrates where data are rich; LLM ideation converges; individual exploration may be rising; and one synthesis finds the evidence on novelty "ambiguous or negative" (Castellacci et al. 2026). ### 4b. The hypothesis

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4b.1 Metric incentives channel AI into exploitation; a hypothesis others now share · "I reached it independently" deleted (R1-1, R2-2d); "well founded" → "has support" (R1 minor 8); the 2026 Nature pieces the brief names added (Messeri & Crockett 2026; Motoki 2026) -->
The hypothesis is that scientists use AI to do faster what they already know how to do, because count-based metrics with short time windows reward exactly that. [AUTHOR: two sentences, in your own voice, on where the hypothesis came from; no claim of priority.] Others now state it. Huang et al. (2026) write that "AI makes extending a familiar research line faster and cheaper than entering unfamiliar terrain, and existing incentive systems reward such speed"; and Messeri & Crockett now call the uncritical adoption of AI in science alarming (Messeri & Crockett 2026 [RECHECK: title only; not audited]; see also Motoki 2026 [RECHECK: title only; not audited]). The mechanism has support: novel papers are less cited in short windows (Wang et al. 2017), and evaluators score novel proposals lower (Boudreau et al. 2016), whereas journal editors select for novelty (Teplitskiy et al. 2022). The bias sits in short-window metrics and grant panels, where AI-cheapened output is counted; that AI is being channelled by it is the hypothesis.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4b.2 The rival: affordance, plus selection · Hao et al. quoted exactly (audit R34); incentives were not measured (R73); Huang et al.'s three remedies (R38) -->
The rival explanation needs no incentives. AI works best on well-posed, data-rich problems, so it would yield exploitation even under neutral evaluation. In Hao et al.'s supplementary analyses, data availability "appears to be a major impacting factor" in where AI concentrates, while topicality, original impact and funding priority "remain almost unrelated" to it; incentives were not measured. Heavy users may also have been different all along (Zheng et al. 2026). Huang et al. blend the first two mechanisms, and their three remedies map onto them: subsidising data infrastructure in neglected domains answers affordance, while ending pivot penalties and rewarding "the conceptual distance between a researcher's current and previous work" answer incentives.

### 4c. Governance as rationing

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4c.1 Reform as an ally (R1-6): AI strengthens the case for reading over counting, but reform depends on the capacity that volume consumes (draft 1's last sentence of ¶4c.4, rescued) · CoARA count and EC wording corrected (audit R39, O-03); Registered Reports and Curry 2018 dropped (R1-6d) · China (the former ¶4c.4) is now one clause here, with its verified sources (CN1, R48); 3,800: drop that clause -->
Reform that changes what is counted exists, and AI strengthens its case. DORA (2012) and CoARA (2022) ask evaluators to assess researchers by reading their work, with metrics in support. CoARA's agreement had 994 listed signatories on 16 October 2026, Utrecht University is among the institutions that changed practice (Woolston 2021), and a 2026 study for the European Commission found engagement "broad and growing" but progress uneven (European Commission 2026). A panel that reads three papers per candidate is not swamped when output doubles. [AUTHOR: say whether EMBL and UPF have signed DORA or CoARA; check before writing.] But reform depends on the resource that AI-era volume consumes. <!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4c.2 Rationing defined; caps, gates and sanctions separated (R1-3a,b) · conference rules from the venues' own calls (audit R41, R80–R83); the ban attributed to its primary source (R40) · 3,800: a compact list · SocArXiv's cap left to Table 1, and ACL's lottery too (length) -->
Against this background, the responses with teeth have been limits (Table 1). By rationing I mean a limit on how many items a person may submit in a period, applied whatever the quality of the work or the conduct of its author. NIH's six applications a year and arXiv's two submissions a month since 1 October 2026 are rationing in this sense (NIH 2025; arXiv 2026). So are the quotas of AI conferences. ICLR 2027 allows each author 20 submissions but at most one paper on which no author has had an acceptance at a major machine-learning venue (ICLR 2027). Two measures differ in kind. arXiv's peer-review requirement for computer-science reviews is a gate (arXiv 2025a), and the one-year ban that the chair of arXiv's computer-science section announced in May 2026 for authors who did not check their AI output is a sanction aimed at misuse (Dietterich 2026, quoted in Ha 2026).

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4c.2b New in draft 2: whom the caps bind, what they restore, tiering, who rations and who counts (R1-3a,c,d,f); Blekhman's "no brakes" answered (author's decision) -->
Most of these caps sit far above what typical researchers submit, so for now they bind only the tail, as NIH expects of its own cap; even at the peak, fewer than 3% of authors in arXiv mathematics posted five or more papers in a quarter (Figure 4). A cap restores a little information, since an investigator limited to six applications reveals which six she thinks best, but not what costly production revealed across authors. Blekhman (2026) writes that "there are no brakes on the system". The caps are brakes, crude ones. <!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4c.3 Rationing versus pricing versus redesign · "refundable deposits" restored and the equity point sourced: both are in the full text of Hanneke et al. 2026 (KIT repository copy; research note 7), which resolves audit §4.3 and the [CITATION NEEDED]; Fourie cited for diagnosis only, not as a proposer of fees (note 7) -->
Rationing is one of four answers to cheap output. Pricing is the second. Fourie (2026) diagnoses review as "rationed, not priced"; Atasu (2026) and Hanneke et al. (2026) propose submission fees, some contingent on AI use, as refundable deposits or with waivers, and pay for reviewers. <!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4c.3b New in draft 2: the fourth answer, automated evaluation (R1-3e; the brief's AI triage, detectors and bans on AI in review) · Xuegong Zhang's "AI support, not AI bans" moved to the Section 6 seeds (16 Oct; length) -->
The fourth answer is to meet machines with machines, and institutions are most likely to adopt it. Its parts are visible already: detectors to police AI text, which Chinese universities already apply to theses (CCTV 2026); language models at the front line of review (Bhargava et al. 2026) [RECHECK: abstract only; not audited] or AI screening at inclusion (Hansen 2026); and bans on AI in review itself (NIH 2023). If AI cheapened evaluation as fast as production, volume would be manageable. But a judgement that a model makes about text a model helped to write measures what models find plausible. Automated triage can sort for integrity and fit; it cannot supply the judgement whose scarcity the caps conceal.

<!-- KEEP-3800 --><!-- ¶4c.5 The responses sort into four kinds; none changes how researchers are counted (R1 minor 11, 12) · the bibliometric counter-pull named (R1-6c); Blekhman's prestige fallback vs rationing (author's decision) -->
So the responses sort into four kinds. Disclosure rules govern conduct (Thorp 2023; Ganjavi et al. 2024), caps ration volume, automation is arriving, and reform of what counts stays voluntary. None changes how researchers are counted for hiring, promotion or funding, so evaluation falls back on what it has. Blekhman (2026) expects the fallback to be journal prestige; rationing is a second fallback, already under way, and by keeping counts meaningful a little longer it lowers the pressure to stop counting. And the longer caps keyed to past acceptances last, the more they favour the incumbents whose agreement any change would need.

Narrowing therefore has candidate drivers at three levels. What researchers are rewarded for is the internal driver (Section 4). ## Box 2 | How this hypothesis could be tested

The hypothesis is that metric incentives channel generative AI into exploitation (Huang et al. 2026). I have found no test of it, and Hao et al.'s data cannot settle it (Section 4a).

<!-- KEEP-3800 -->
A triple difference by the intensity of metric incentives could discriminate between incentives and the pull of data-rich problems, under stated assumptions. Within scientists, compare exploration before and after November 2022 by intensity of AI use, and ask whether the effect is larger where counting bites harder. The main variation should come from dated changes in what is counted: for example, institutions in one country and field that adopted CoARA action plans at known dates, compared with similar institutions that did not, with selection into signing modelled. Career stage is a weaker moderator, because AI use itself changes it: in Hao et al.'s data, AI users lead projects 1.37 years earlier. Comparisons across countries must condition on language: publication-counting regimes are common where English is not the first language, which is also where detectors misfire (Liang et al. 2023) and where exploration rose most after 2022 (Zheng et al. 2026). Data availability should be measured by topic, for example as the share of a topic's papers that use public datasets, and interacted with AI use. AI use should come from disclosure statements, coded by task, with detectors only as a check.
