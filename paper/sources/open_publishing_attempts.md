# Open, review-centred, web-native publishing: what has been tried

*Research note for the PLOS ONE article, compiled 17 October 2026 (sources opened that day unless stated). Not yet read by the author.*

*Independent spot-check by the coordinator (17 October 2026, 14:40 UTC), against the primary sources: eLife's submission counts (2,853 in 2025, 6,396 in 2024; 1,013 and 1,719 sent for review); Brodeur et al. 2026 (Nature 652(8108):151–156, 347 authors on Crossref); and AAAI-26 (reviews for "all 22,977 full-review papers in less than a day", arXiv abstract). All three match.*

**Labels.** **V**: checked in the primary source (for an initiative's own statistics, V means "checked as its own claim", not audited). **R**: reported by a named secondary source. **NV**: not verified. **Own**: my count from public metadata, method stated. Inferences are marked "(inference)". Every quotation was checked against the saved source text.

## 1. Verdict

- **Every component has been tried, but never together, and none at scale.** These all exist:
  - publication before review with public reports (Copernicus since 2001, F1000Research since 2012, eLife since 2023);
  - reviews as citable outputs (SciPost, Octopus, MetaROR, The Unjournal);
  - web-native articles (Distill, eLife's executable articles, JoVE, IPOL, AGU notebooks).

  No initiative combines them, and no major funder or university credits public critiques as a primary output.
- **"Nothing is accepted" has survived only with a gate somewhere.**
  - eLife sends 27–35% of submissions for review.
  - F1000Research has no editor, but databases index only articles with enough "Approved" reports.
  - Web of Science indexes only a "validated" subset of such journals and withholds the Impact Factor.

  The gate moves; it does not disappear.
- **The best-measured outcome is the penalty.** After eLife lost its Impact Factor, its submissions fell by 55% from 2024 to 2025, and by 80% from China. eLife says quality and finances held; that is its own assessment.
- **Unsolicited public criticism is rare wherever it has been measured.**
  - 5% of authors opted into Nature's 2006 trial.
  - PubMed Commons closed after comments on 6,000 of 28 million articles.
  - 7.4% of PLOS articles and 7.3% of 2020 bioRxiv/medRxiv preprints drew comments.
  - In my count, 7–16% of Copernicus discussion papers drew a community comment.

  The exception, PubPeer, runs on anonymity and on misconduct detection by a few heavy users.
- **Credit for critiques is the weakest link.** Reviews are recorded (Web of Science, ORCID, 1.1 million Crossref peer-review records) but seldom counted.
  - 36% of top universities mention peer review in promotion criteria.
  - EMBO excludes "reviews posted by individuals".
  - A cash bounty for finding errors could not recruit enough reviewers.
- **Organised verification with conventional credit has worked**: crowd reproductions of 110 articles, published in *Nature* with 347 authors, and JOSS's open software review.
- **Generative AI cuts against the proposal at its centre.** An estimated 21% of reviews at one 2026 machine-learning conference were fully AI-generated. Another conference produced an AI review for each of 22,977 papers in under a day. If critiques become the credited unit, their numbers will be gamed as paper counts were, unless credit attaches to consequences that can be checked (inference).

## 2. Initiatives

### A. Publishing without accept/reject decisions

**A1. eLife (publish–review–curate since January 2023).** eLife reviews only preprints. Every article it reviews is published, with public reviews and an eLife Assessment: "if an article is peer reviewed by eLife, the authors know that it will be published" ([Behrens et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.110392)).
- *Indexing.* Web of Science put eLife on hold on 23 October 2024 and dropped it from the Science Citation Index Expanded on 12 November. It now indexes eLife in ESCI, but only articles rated "solid" or better ([Oct](https://elifesciences.org/inside-elife/16afe6ec/update-on-elife-s-indexing-status-at-web-of-science), [Nov](https://elifesciences.org/inside-elife/c11c6101/the-elife-model-an-update-on-progress-following-changes-in-web-of-science-indexing-status), [Dec 2024](https://elifesciences.org/inside-elife/ae620829/changes-to-elife-s-indexing-status-in-web-of-science-and-scopus)). Clarivate's rule: "Partially indexed journals/platforms will not be eligible for a Journal Impact Factor™ (JIF) or any other journal-level citation metric" ([Clarivate](https://clarivate.com/academia-government/scientific-and-academic-research/research-discovery-and-referencing/web-of-science/web-of-science-core-collection/editorial-selection-process/editorial-policies-category-change-requests-appeals/)). Its rationale: "Allowing cover-to-cover indexing of such journals would mean indexing content that is unsound and risks allowing untrustworthy actors to benefit from publishing poor-quality content" ([Research Professional News](https://www.researchprofessionalnews.com/rr-news-world-2024-10-elife-to-lose-impact-factor-due-to-peer-review-change/); this source gives the last JIF as 6.4, R). Scopus moved eLife to its preprint collection.
- *Submissions* ([three-year update](https://elifesciences.org/inside-elife/7da92a51/the-elife-model-three-year-update)):
  - 6,396 in 2024 and 2,853 in 2025 (−55%);
  - sent for review: 1,719, then 1,013 (27%, then 35%, of submissions);
  - by country: UK −18%, US −40%, Germany −52%, Italy −75%, China −80%;
  - output: 143 articles a month, then 84.

  The editorial concedes: "We also underestimated the commercial forces that the scientific community as a whole will need to overcome to enact any meaningful change in publishing."
- *2026.*
  - The legacy track is closing to new submissions.
  - An AI partnership (qed science) aims to "enhance the discussion around articles".
  - Reviewers will be able to be named "so that they can receive public recognition for their work".
  - Explainer videos can be embedded in figures.
  - There is a new Replication Studies article type.
  - Wellcome is giving £2.4m.

  In 2025, over 100 institutions and funders ("over 95% of respondents", a self-selected group) said they still consider eLife papers.
- *Confidence:* V (self-reported figures).

**A2. F1000Research and funder platforms.** F1000Research (2012; Taylor & Francis since 2020): "There is no Editor (or Editor-in-Chief) to make a decision on whether to accept or reject the article".
- *Review and indexing.* Authors suggest reviewers, who are named, and each report gets a DOI. PubMed and Scopus index an article only after two "Approved", or one "Approved" and two "Approved with Reservations" ([about](https://f1000research.com/about)).
- *Outcomes, 2012–2017.* 80% of 1,865 articles were indexed, but "The peer review for about 15% of the articles had either been ongoing for more than a year or discontinued completely". Some authors felt "trapped". Some suspected that named reviewers chosen by authors hold back criticism ([Kirkham & Moher 2018](https://doi.org/10.12688/f1000research.15436.1)).
- *Funder platforms.* Wellcome Open Research (2016), Gates Open Research (2017) and Open Research Europe (2021) use the model ([F1000](https://f1000.com/about/)).
  - *Gates Open Research.* New articles fell from 120 in 2019 to 31 in 2025 (Own: Crossref version-1 DOIs). Gates had moved to preprints and stated "The Foundation Will Not Pay Article Processing Charges (APC)" ([policy](https://openaccess.gatesfoundation.org/open-access-policy/)); that this caused the fall is inference.
  - *Open Research Europe.* It has published "more than 1,200 articles". From autumn 2026 CERN runs it, with "a nearly €17 million budget for the period 2026-2031" ([European Commission](https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/news/all-research-and-innovation-news/new-era-open-research-europe-2026-03-26_en)).
- *Confidence:* V.

**A3. Copernicus/EGU public discussion (ACP, 2001).** Screened submissions are posted for comment by referees and anyone else, then revised and accepted or rejected. ACP's founder wrote that "about one in five papers receives a comment from the scientific community in addition to the comments from designated referees" ([Pöschl 2012](https://doi.org/10.3389/fncom.2012.00033); self-reported).
- *Own count.* Crossref peer-review records, prefix 10.5194. Suffix -rc marks a referee comment; -sc or -cc marks a community comment. Denominator: discussion papers with at least one referee comment.
  - ACP: 9–11% drew a community comment in 2016–2020, and 7–8% in 2021–2022.
  - All Copernicus journals: 11–16% a year in 2016–2025 (653 of 4,485 in 2024).
- *Confidence:* Own (assumes the comment DOIs are complete).

**A4. Review Commons (EMBO Press and ASAPbio, December 2019).** It reviews preprints independently of journals and, since June 2022, posts the reports on bioRxiv or medRxiv. Journals still decide; affiliates grew from 17 to 28 ([launch](https://www.embo.org/press-releases/review-commons-a-pre-journal-portable-review-platform/); [policy](https://www.reviewcommons.org/blog/new-policy-review-commons-makes-preprint-peer-review-fully-transparent/); [about](https://www.reviewcommons.org/about/)). By March 2021 it had reviewed "nearly 500 manuscripts" and "mediated the publication of 128 papers" ([blog](https://www.reviewcommons.org/blog/will-refereed-preprints-transform-scientific-communication/)); I found no later totals (NV). EMBO (not EMBL) counts a first-author refereed preprint as a paper for its postdoctoral fellowships, but "Informal comments, highlights, and reviews posted by individuals are excluded" ([EMBO 2022](https://www.embo.org/features/refereed-preprints-recognized-as-eligibility-criterion-for-embo-postdoctoral-fellowships/)). *Confidence:* V.

**A5. Peer Community In (2017).** Recommenders organise reviews and then recommend a preprint or not, so a gate remains. In 2025 it had 521 submissions and 248 recommendations, and its diamond journal, now in Web of Science, published 140 articles ([highlights](https://peercommunityin.org/2026/01/14/pci-and-peer-community-journal-highlights-of-2025)). Its 516 k€ of resources included 215 k€ of salaries donated in kind ([finances](https://peercommunityin.org/2026/01/12/2025-finances-article-costs/)). *Confidence:* V (self-reported).

**A6. PREreview (2017) and Sciety (eLife, 2021).** PREreview's homepage lists "2,185 PREreviews" by "4,761 PREreviewers" ([prereview.org](https://prereview.org/en-us)). eLife reported 36,000 preprints with 76,000 evaluations from 27 initiatives on Sciety by the end of 2024 ([eLife](https://elifesciences.org/inside-elife/330eff62/a-look-back-at-2024)). *Confidence:* V (self-reported).

**A7. MetaROR (RoRI and AIMOS, November 2024).** In this venue for metascience, "the MetaROR editor won’t make a binary accept/reject decision", and reports "constitute scholarly contributions in their own right" ([RoRI](https://researchonresearch.org/introducing-metaror/); [MetaROR](https://metaror.org/)). *Uptake:* NV.

**A8. The Unjournal (first evaluation 2023).** Paid, public, rated evaluations of economics and social-science papers, with DOIs; it publishes no papers. It reports "$300+ per evaluation", "100+ Evaluations completed" and "55+ Research papers assessed" ([about](https://info.unjournal.org/about)). *Confidence:* V (self-reported).

**A9. SciPost (2016).** Physics journals with public reports that carry DOIs (8,558 Crossref records; Own). Acceptance remains, and sponsors pay instead of authors. In May 2025 its founder warned: "Only 147 out of 1351 (a mere 11%) benefitting organizations have sponsored us". He named author fees or closure as the alternatives ([Caux](https://jscaux.org/blog/post/2025/05/13/scipost-at-a-crossroads/)); I found no 2026 resolution. In 2025 it launched a Journal of Robustness Reports for reanalyses ([editorial](https://doi.org/10.21468/JRobustRep.0-Editorial)). *Confidence:* V.

**A10. Qeios.** Named public reviews with star ratings; "Our custom-built AI identifies potential reviewers among qualified researchers with field expertise" ([policy](https://www.qeios.com/publishing-policy)). It is Crossref's sixth-largest depositor of peer-review records, with 57,577 (Own). Researchers on forums call its invitations spam (R, anecdotal). *Review quality:* NV.

**A11. Octopus (UK, 2022).** Research is split into eight publication types, including the review. A 2025 review found "only four peer reviews of publications on the site" ([Katina](https://katinamagazine.org/content/article/resource-reviews/2025/open-access-publishing-platform-researchers-attention)). Its API now lists 11 peer-review versions among 28,621 publication versions (Own, [API](https://prod.api.octopus.ac/v1/publication-versions?type=PEER_REVIEW)). About 7,000 records in each of four other types suggests bulk seeding (inference).

**A12. ResearchEquals (Liberate Science, 2022).** Modular outputs with DOIs. On 1 September 2026 its founder wrote that "there is no clear pathway to sustainability for ResearchEquals right now". Five institutions give €15k a year in total, and the rest is volunteer work ([post](https://libscie.org/3muh3hzjaj22v)). *Confidence:* V.

**A13. Funders.**
- *cOAlition S.*
  - *Proposal.* "Towards Responsible Publishing" (31 October 2023) proposed that "Peer Review Editors then provide an editorʼs evaluation or summary of the reviewing process, but do not make any recommendation as to whether the article is suitable for publication" ([proposal](https://www.coalition-s.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Towards_Responsible_Publishing_web.pdf)).
  - *Consultation.* Over 11,600 respondents supported preprints and open review ([July 2024](https://www.coalition-s.org/coalition-s-welcomes-the-findings-from-the-consultation-on-the-towards-responsible-publishing-proposal/)). I could not find the funders' promised response.
  - *Strategy (November 2025).* It plans to "Review and update the Plan S principles". It notes that researchers will adopt these practices "provided they are adequately recognised and rewarded" ([strategy](https://www.coalition-s.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/cOAlition-S-Strategy-2026-2030-full-version.pdf)).
  - No binding policy has followed (inference).
- *HHMI (since January 2026).* For internal reviews, "a preprint version of the scientist’s choosing - not any subsequent journal publication - will generally serve as the official version of the article" ([policy](https://hhmicdn.blob.core.windows.net/policies/Immediate-Access-to-Research.pdf)).
- *Gates:* see A2.
- *Confidence:* V.

### B. Open public commenting

**B1. Nature's open peer review trial (June–September 2006).** Of 1,369 papers sent for review, "The authors of 71 (or 5%) of these agreed" to open display. Of those, "33 received no comments, while 38 (54%) received a total of 92 technical comments", and 49 of the comments went to 8 papers. The report concluded: "Feedback suggests that there is a marked reluctance among researchers to offer open comments" ([report](https://blogs.nature.com/blog/report_of_natures_peer_review_trial/)). Since June 2025 Nature has published reviewer reports for all new papers; reviewers stay anonymous and accept/reject is unchanged ([Springer Nature](https://group.springernature.com/fr/group/media/press-releases/transparent-peer-review-now-standard-for-nature/27788498)). *Confidence:* V.

**B2. PubMed Commons (2013–2018).** Only authors of PubMed records could join ([NCBI 2013](https://ncbiinsights.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2013/10/23/joining-pubmed-commons-a-step-by-step-guide/)). NIH closed it because "usage has remained minimal, with comments submitted on only 6,000 of the 28 million articles indexed in PubMed". It added that "the low level of participation does not warrant continued investment in the project, particularly given the availability of other commenting venues" ([NCBI 2018](https://ncbiinsights.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2018/02/01/pubmed-commons-to-be-discontinued/)). *Confidence:* V.

**B3. PubPeer (2012).** Moderated comments, anonymous or signed, on any article with a DOI. In 2024 the Einstein Foundation reported "over 300,000 comments" and "at least 5500 anonymous and 1000 signed comments posted a month". It estimated that 19% of retractions since 2012 had prior PubPeer discussion (R, [award](https://award.einsteinfoundation.de/award-winners-finalists/recipients-2024/pubpeer)). In a 2019–2020 sample, over two-thirds of comments reported possible misconduct, mainly image manipulation, and "super commenters" screened systematically ([Ortega 2022](https://doi.org/10.1002/asi.24568)). *Confidence:* R/V.

**B4. PLOS comments.** For 2003–2016, "only 7.4% of articles published across all PLOS journals have been commented upon" (5.2% without publisher accounts), and the rate declined after 2010 ([Wakeling et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0165551518819965); [summary](https://scholarlykitchen.sspnet.org/2019/05/15/guest-post-a-study-of-commenting-on-plos-articles/)). *Confidence:* V.

**B5. bioRxiv/medRxiv comments.** Of 52,736 preprints from 2020, 7.3% had a comment after a mean of 7.5 months; the median comment was 43 words long ([Carneiro et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.31410)). Among bioRxiv preprints with a single comment, 12% of non-author comments were full review reports ([Malički et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.11613/BM.2021.020201)). *Confidence:* V.

**B6. OpenReview at ICLR.** Reviews and discussions are public. Over 80% of 2,385 surveyed machine-learning researchers supported public comments, but feared resubmission bias and the unmasking of reviewers ([Rao et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2511.23439)). In November 2025 a bug exposed identities. ICLR then found "a malicious commenter posting public comments on 600 papers identifying the reviewers", and third parties harassing and bribing reviewers, and it froze public comments ([ICLR](https://blog.iclr.cc/2025/12/03/iclr-2026-response-to-security-incident/)). *Confidence:* V.

**B7. alphaXiv (2024).** It launched as a comment layer for arXiv (R). Its homepage now leads with AI-generated "grounded" literature answers ([alphaxiv.org](https://www.alphaxiv.org/); Own reading). *Usage:* NV.

**B8. Hypothesis.** eLife replaced its comments with Hypothesis annotation in January 2018 ([eLife](https://elifesciences.org/for-the-press/81d42f7d)). I found no uptake study for research articles (NV).

### C. Credit for reviews and critiques

**C1. Review records.**
- *Publons.* Clarivate bought Publons in June 2017, when "more than 150,000 reviewers" used it ([press release](https://prnewswire.com/in/news-releases/clarivate-analytics-acquires-market-leader-publons-creating-the-definitive-publisher-independent-platform-for-accelerating-research-through-peer-review-625690273.html)). It is now Web of Science Reviewer Recognition, "operating on over 5,000 scholarly journals" ([ACM](https://authors.acm.org/author-resources/publons)).
- *ORCID.* It held 535,472 peer-review items in September 2018, 512,700 of them from Publons ([ORCID](https://info.orcid.org/whats-new-with-peer-review-on-orcid/)).
- *Crossref.* It has registered peer reviews since 2017 (Own, [API](https://api.crossref.org/types/peer-review/works?rows=0)):
  - all years: 1,134,786 records against 124.5 million journal articles (0.9%);
  - 2025: 233,176 against 6.65 million (3.5%).
- *Confidence:* V/Own.

**C2. Does reviewing count?**
- *CoARA (2022).* Assessors should "Consider tasks like peer review, training, mentoring and supervision of Ph.D candidates, leadership roles" ([agreement](https://www.eua.eu/downloads/news/2022_07_19_rra_agreement_final.pdf)).
- *DORA.* It expects that "the peer-reviewed research paper will remain a central research output that informs research assessment" ([DORA](https://sfdora.org/read/)).
- *Promotion criteria.* Of 42 top universities in the QS 2025 ranking, "Only 15 (36%)" name peer review as a promotion criterion ([El-Sobky et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2026.1859654)). In seven countries, open and responsible practices are "minimally rewarded" ([Pontika et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1162/qss_a_00224)).
- *National rules.* I found none that credits public critiques (search not exhaustive).
- *Confidence:* V.

**C3. Paying for reviews.**
- *Critical Care Medicine.* A $250 offer raised completed reviews from 42.2% to 49.8% of invitations, and quality did not change ([Cotton et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1097/CCM.0000000000006637)).
- *ResearchHub.* It pays "the equivalent of US$150 per review" in its own cryptocurrency ([Else 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-024-04027-4)).
- *ERROR (Bern, 2024).* A bug bounty: 250–1,000 Swiss francs per review, plus up to 2,500 for errors found. It "can’t find enough reviewers to do the work", so it will now publish the reviews in a journal ([Science 2026](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aeh8588), quoted from the excerpt at [bioethics.com](https://bioethics.com/?p=102095); original not opened).
- *Confidence:* V/R.

**C4. Credited verification.** The Institute for Replication reproduced 110 economics and political-science articles from journals that require data and code: "more than 85% of published claims were computationally reproducible", and 72% of significant estimates survived robustness checks. The *Nature* paper has 347 authors ([Brodeur et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10251-x)). SciPost (A9) and eLife (A1) now also publish reanalyses as citable articles. *Confidence:* V.

### D. Web-native articles

**D1. Distill (2017–2021).** Interactive machine-learning articles. It has been on hiatus since 2 July 2021 ([announcement](https://doi.org/10.23915/distill.00031)) and has published nothing since September 2021 ([distill.pub](https://distill.pub/)). The editors gave three reasons:
- burnout: "Distill is volunteer run and these frictions have caused our team to struggle with burnout";
- credit: "we don’t think that publishing in a journal like Distill significantly affects how seriously most institutions take non-traditional publications";
- cost: "the amount of effort it takes to produce these articles", with "more than 50 hours" of help for early ones.

They added that "reviewers are willing to spend orders of magnitude more energy when they feel like reviewing a paper genuinely matters to the community, rather than being pro-forma, and their work will be seen as a scientific contribution." *Confidence:* V.

**D2. eLife Executable Research Articles (2020).** Live code and interactive figures, built with Stencila, for eLife papers that were already published ([launch](https://elifesciences.org/labs/dc5acbde/welcome-to-a-new-era-of-reproducible-publishing)). By March 2021, "There are currently six published ERA articles" ([eLife Labs](https://elifesciences.org/labs/a04d2b80/announcing-the-next-phase-of-executable-research-articles)). I found no later count (NV). eLife's 2026 step is video, not code (A1).

**D3. AGU Notebooks Now! and Curvenote (Sloan grant, 2022).** Computational notebooks as peer-reviewed articles in *Earth and Space Science*. The site still describes a pilot in which "not all submissions will be chosen" ([notebooks.agu.org](https://notebooks.agu.org/)). Number published: NV. The underlying tools, Jupyter Book and MyST, are active (R: project blog).

**D4. JoVE (2006).** Video methods articles. JoVE launched open access but moved to subscriptions in 2009 because of video costs: "We like open access, we just can't survive on it" ([The Scientist](https://www.the-scientist.com/video-journal-goes-closed-access-44231)). "Standard" access still requires an institutional subscription ([JoVE](https://help.jove.com/hc/en-us/articles/31079436357780-Access-and-Publication-Policies)). *Confidence:* V.

**D5. IPOL (2011).** Articles with peer-reviewed code and an online demo; 298 Crossref records in all, 9 in 2025 (Own).

**D6. Living Reviews (1998).** Reviews that their authors keep updated, from the Max Planck Institute for Gravitational Physics; sold to Springer in 2015 ([MPG](https://www.mpg.de/9293322/living-reviews-springer)). How often they are updated: NV.

**D7. Elsevier's "Article of the Future" (2009).** Redesigned online articles linked to data ([Aalbersberg et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1629/2048-7754.25.1.33)). Whether it displaced the PDF: NV.

**D8. arXiv HTML (2023).** Every new TeX submission is converted automatically to accessible HTML. About 75% convert without errors, and the project "remains experimental" ([Ginev et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2605.16562); [Frankston et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2402.08954)). It scales because authors do nothing extra (inference).

**D9. Data, replication and software journals.**
- *GigaByte (2020).* 11–42 articles a year, 26 in 2025 (Own).
- *ReScience C (2015).* 1–12 replications a year until 2019, 34–54 a year in 2020–2024, then 7 in 2025 ([ReScience](https://rescience.github.io/read/)). The bulge probably reflects organised challenges (inference, NV).
- *JOSS (2016).* "Submission and review proceed in the open, on GitHub". It has published 400–550 papers a year recently (Own) and works "within the current merit system of science" ([Smith et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj-cs.147)).

## 3. Lessons

1. **Participation.** Unsolicited comment reaches roughly 5–16% of papers and tends to fall over time (B1, B2, B4, B5, A3, A11). It concentrates on a few papers (B1) and a few people (B3). PubPeer, the active exception, is mostly anonymous and focused on misconduct.
2. **Reviewer burden.** Without an editor recruiting reviewers, review stalls (A2). Bounties do not fix recruitment, and payment brings modest gains (C3). Volunteer editing burns out (D1).
3. **Credit not counted.** Review records exist (C1) but are seldom used in evaluation (C2). Funders credit organised review, not individual critiques (A4), and their consultation made adoption conditional on reward (A13). Distill found that a venue did not change institutional valuation (D1). The successes issue conventional credit, papers and authorship (C4, D9).
4. **Sustainability.** Volunteer and grant-funded platforms struggle (A5, A9, A12, D1). The Center for Open Science is cutting back OSF Projects: "63% of OSF projects, more than 650,000, have never been made public". Keeping the full service would need $4–5 million a year, of which recurring revenue covers 10–20% ([COS 2026](https://www.cos.io/blog/osf-changes-a-note-to-users)). JoVE survived behind a paywall (D4). Living Reviews and F1000 passed to commercial publishers (D6, A2).
5. **Prestige and indexing.** The indexer's gate is binary and costly to refuse (A1). The penalty was largest where evaluation is metric-driven (China −80%) and smallest in the UK (−18%).
6. **Moderation and abuse.** Visible identities invited harassment and collusion (B6). AI adds new attacks: hidden instructions in manuscripts steer AI reviewers ([Gibney 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-025-02172-y)).
7. **Format without incentive.** Formats that need extra author effort stayed small (D1–D3, D5). Those that need none (D8), or that bring credit or revenue (D4, D9), persist.

## 4. The AI problem for this proposal

- **AI-written reviews are already common.**
  - Liang et al. estimated that 6.5–16.9% of review text at four 2023–24 machine-learning conferences "could have been substantially modified by LLMs". The share was higher in low-confidence reviews and in reviews submitted near the deadline ([ICML 2024, PMLR 235:29575–620](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/liang24b.html)).
  - A detector study estimated that at least 15.8% of ICLR 2024 reviews were AI-assisted, and that these reviews gave higher scores ([Latona et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2405.02150), preprint).
  - For ICLR 2026, a detection company estimated that 21% of reviews were fully AI-generated (R: [Naddaf 2025a](https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-025-03506-6)).
  - ICLR ran two detectors on all 76,139 reviews, conceding that their "accuracy is far from perfect", and removes repeatedly flagged reviewers ([ICLR](https://blog.iclr.cc/2026/03/31/a-retrospective-on-the-iclr-2026-review-process/)).
  - In a Frontiers survey of about 1,600 researchers, over 50% had used AI while reviewing (R: [Naddaf 2025b](https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-025-04066-5)).
- **AI critique is nearly free.** AAAI-26 produced an AI review for each of 22,977 papers "in less than a day", and survey respondents found them useful ([Biswas et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2604.13940), organisers' preprint). bioRxiv and medRxiv offer authors an AI report "typically produced within about 30 minutes" ([bioRxiv](https://connect.biorxiv.org/news/2025/11/04/qed_review_tool)); it comes from the same company as eLife's partner.
- **Quality is uneven.**
  - *Serious errors.* On 91 errors serious enough to prompt errata or retraction, no model exceeded 21.1% recall or 6.1% precision ([Son et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2505.11855), preprint).
  - *Minor errors.* Experts confirmed 263 of 316 objective, mostly minor mistakes that an AI checker flagged in published AI papers ([Bianchi et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2512.05925), preprint).
  - *Expert evaluation.* A one-shot AI evaluation found on average 80% of the consensus issues raised by paid Unjournal evaluators in 12 papers. Its authors say their uncertainties "prevent an equivalence claim" ([Klotzbücher et al. 2026](https://valentinklotzbuecher.github.io/llm-uj-research-eval/), working paper).
- **Policy.**
  - *NIH.* It prohibits reviewers "from using natural language processors, large language models, or other generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies for analyzing and formulating peer review critiques for grant applications and R&D contract proposals" ([NOT-OD-23-149](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-23-149.html)). The ground is confidentiality, which would not apply to public critiques of public preprints (inference).
  - *ICLR.* Reviewers who posted false or hallucinated content had their own papers desk-rejected ([ICLR](https://blog.iclr.cc/2025/11/19/iclr-2026-response-to-llm-generated-papers-and-reviews/)).
- **PubPeer.** I found no measured evidence of AI-generated comments on PubPeer or similar sites; that does not prove they are absent.
- **Do code and data make verification cheaper?** They make it possible, not automatic:
  - 26% of sampled *Science* papers were reproduced after requests to authors ([Stodden et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1708290115));
  - 74% of R files in Harvard Dataverse failed to run ([Trisovic et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-022-01143-6));
  - 879 of 22,578 Python notebooks linked to PubMed Central papers reran to identical results ([Samuel & Mietchen 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/gigascience/giad113));
  - with mandatory sharing and organised reproducers, more than 85% of claims reproduced (C4).
- **Implication (inference).**
  - *Openness helps detection.* ICLR said public reviews let the community "see issues that are only visible at scale" ([ICLR](https://blog.iclr.cc/2025/11/19/iclr-2026-response-to-llm-generated-papers-and-reviews/)).
  - *Counts will inflate.* If critiques become the credited unit, cheap AI critiques will inflate their number, as they inflate papers.
  - *What deserves credit.* Critiques whose value is checkable and has consequences: a reproduction that reruns code, an error confirmed by authors or editors, or a correction or retraction that follows.

## 5. Draft paragraph for the Discussion

Blekhman argues that generative AI has broken the paper as evidence of research, and that the field must "define new ways to evaluate science that are aligned with its goals"; he names no mechanism [1]. One concrete mechanism would publish work as web pages carrying data and code, make no accept/reject decision, and credit public critiques rather than articles. Each part has been tried. eLife stopped rejecting reviewed papers in 2023 and lost its Journal Impact Factor; its submissions then fell by 55% between 2024 and 2025 [2,3]. Open commenting has rarely drawn participants. Only 5% of authors joined Nature's 2006 trial, and 33 of the 71 papers displayed received no comment [4]. PubMed Commons closed after comments on 6,000 of 28 million articles [5], and 7.3% of 2020 bioRxiv and medRxiv preprints received a comment [6]. Distill suspended its interactive journal partly because the venue did not change how institutions valued such work [7]. Verification fared better: reproductions of 110 economics and political-science articles, published with 347 authors, found over 85% of claims reproducible [8]. The main risk is that AI makes critiques cheap. About 21% of reviews at one 2026 machine-learning conference were estimated to be fully AI-generated [9]. Another conference produced an AI review for each of 22,977 papers in under a day [10]. Critiques deserve credit only when their value can be checked, as with reproductions, errors confirmed by authors or editors, and the corrections that follow.

## 6. What Blekhman proposes (PDF in this folder)

He names no mechanism. His programme, verbatim:
- "Therefore, we have to be deliberate about establishing a new way to evaluate biology research. The response cannot be that we stop evaluating ourselves."
- "Instead, we could use this surge to acknowledge that paper counts are meaningless, and, as a field, abandon the current incentive system and return our focus to the substance of research."

He presents his five goals as "my own first pass at it, offered as a starting point for a conversation". The words "preprint", "credit" and "reward" do not occur in his article. Peer review appears only in his account of arXiv's 2025 rule, among responses that "address symptoms rather than the underlying problem".
