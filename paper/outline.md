## 3. Thesis, contribution and forced wording changes

### Thesis (49 words)
> Academia is not prepared for general AI use. It reads merit from outputs that were costly, implying writing effort, reviewers' time and mastery of a literature. AI, used well, makes them almost free, so the signal fails. Institutions have so far responded by rationing, not by changing what counts.

### Contribution paragraph (169 words; starts from the draft in G6 Q5)
> Recent papers argue that generative AI strains academic evaluation by making its outputs cheap (Manabu 2026; Hansen 2026; Blekhman 2026), and Huang et al. (2026) argue that it accelerates established lines of work rather than opening new ones. I build on them in four ways. First, I locate the failure in legitimate use: signals of merit rest on three scarcities (writing effort, reviewers' time and time to master a literature), and honest use erodes all three (cf. Hanneke et al. 2026). Second, I propose a test that would discriminate between the incentive explanation of narrowing and the rival view that AI simply goes where the data are. Third, extending Fourie's (2026) observation that review is 'rationed, not priced', I read NIH's application cap, arXiv's 2025–26 rules and conference quotas together as rationing that restores scarcity by decree. Fourth, I separate an internal driver of narrowing (incentives) from an external one (corporate control of tools and chat data). The essay is also a documented case of the use it describes.

- No "first" claim. Each contribution names its closest precedent.
- G6 found that P2-test and the P3 synthesis appear new, and that P1 and P4 are new only in emphasis or decomposition. Hence "build on" and "cf. Hanneke".
- If the empirical study goes ahead, add after "data are": "and which I plan to run as a preregistered study [AUTHOR: confirm]".

### Wording changes the evidence forces (one line each)

## 4. Standfirst / abstract (~150 words; the EMBO standfirst would be cut to ~60)

> Generative AI makes papers, reviews and grant applications almost free to produce, and it does so when it is used well, not only when it is abused. Academic evaluation, which reads merit from outputs that used to be costly, is not prepared for this. I argue that legitimacy is irrelevant to the mechanism: honest use erodes the three scarcities on which the signals rest (writing effort, reviewers' time and the time it takes to master a literature). Institutions have so far responded by rationing rather than reform: NIH caps applications, arXiv caps submissions and AI conferences cap authors, restoring scarcity by decree while leaving what is counted unchanged. I distinguish an internal driver of the narrowing that may follow (metric incentives) from an external one (corporate control of tools and chat data), propose a test that discriminates between incentives and the pull of data-rich problems, and document how this essay was itself made with AI agents.

### §1 How the system works (master 550 · 3,800: 150 · 3,500: 100). Shared ground: compress.

**¶1.1 The system converts hard-to-see qualities into counts, and people respond to whatever is counted. [CUT-3500]**
- **Claim:** Evaluation systems convert hard-to-observe qualities into counts, and researchers respond precisely to whatever is counted.
- **Evidence:**
  - Moher et al. 2018: criteria in use are dominated by publication counts, JIF and grant income (L4).
  - Butler 2003: Australia's count-based formula raised output share and lowered relative impact (MEM; re-check).
  - Seeber et al. 2019: Italy's ASN produced "a strong and significant increase in self-citations", 886 scientists (AB).
  - Quan et al. 2017: Chinese cash rewards of US$30–165,000 per Web of Science paper, from 168 documents at 100 universities (AB).
  - Fire & Guestrin 2019: in >120M papers, counts, citations, h-index and JIF have all "ceased to be good" measures (AB).
- **Counter → answer:**
  - *Counter:* Fanelli 2018 calls the "crisis" narrative "mistaken"; Fanelli et al. 2015 find that diffuse pressure does not predict retractions while cash rewards do; Korytkowski & Kulczycki 2019 find that stable Polish incentives raised output with no measured quality drop (SX).
  - *Answer:* claim only "measurably strained, and gameable where incentives are crude" (R's reframing), not "broken".

**¶1.2 Before ChatGPT, output was already outgrowing the capacity to read and review it. [KEEP-3800, one clause in 3,500]**
- **Claim:** Before ChatGPT, output was already outgrowing the capacity to read and review it, so the gates were already the binding constraint.
- **Evidence:**
  - Hanson et al. 2024: indexed articles ~47% higher in 2022 than 2016, outpacing the workforce (AB; workforce figures not re-extracted).
  - Aczel et al. 2021: >100M reviewer-hours in 2020; US reviewers' time worth >US$1.5B (AB).
  - Herbert et al. 2013: 34 working days per NHMRC proposal, 21% funded, ~550 researcher-years a year (AB).
  - Chu & Evans 2021: in large fields a "deluge of papers" ossifies the canon; 1.8B citations, 90M papers (AB).
- **Counter → answer:**
  - *Counter:* growth is old and structural (4.10% a year, Bornmann et al. 2021); reviewer fatigue is overstated (Breuning et al. 2015).
  - *Answer:* the point is not that strain is new. The gates were already the binding constraint, so cheap production hits them first.

**¶1.3 The gates worked because outputs were costly: scarcity-based signals. [KEEP-3800]** This is the pivot paragraph for the whole argument.
- **Claim:** Gatekeepers read merit through three scarcities that made counted outputs costly: writing effort (papers, grant applications), an expert's time (reviews) and years of mastery (syntheses).
- **Evidence:**
  - Cowgill et al. 2026: "Signals are often informative insofar as they require differential knowledge and effort to produce" (AB).
  - Blekhman 2026: paper-based evaluation "took hold because a paper took effort to produce, and that effort served as evidence" (FT).
  - Herbert et al. 2013: 34 days per proposal.
  - arXiv 2026: "Before the advent of AI, there was an easily discernible, practical limit to the rate at which independent submissions could be produced" (PR, OF).
- **Counter → answer:**
  - *Counter:* effort was never a good proxy (Seglen 1997; Brembs et al. 2013), and Hansen calls prestige scarcity "not epistemically necessary".
  - *Answer:* the essay does not defend the proxies. It claims that the system relies on them and that they are failing faster than replacements appear. It also separates **costly-input scarcity** (effort, time) from Hansen's **symbolic scarcity** (prestige by rejection).

**¶1.4 Park et al. on declining disruption: mention only with its critics. [CUT-3800]**
- **Claim:** The often-cited decline in disruptiveness is contested and the baseline does not need it, so it appears only with its critics.
- **Evidence:**
  - Park et al. 2023: the CD index declines (45M papers, 3.9M patents; AB).
  - Contested: Petersen et al. 2024/2025 (citation inflation); Holst et al., *Nature* Matters Arising 2026, "can partially drive"; Park et al. Reply 2026; Macher et al. 2024: "88 percent" of the patent decline comes from truncation (AB).

### §2 AI makes counted output almost free (master 1,100 · 3,800: 700 · 3,500: 650). Legitimate use first; fraud is a secondary amplifier.

**¶2.1 The marginal cost of every counted output fell, and use is already broad and mostly ordinary. [KEEP-3800]**
- **Claim:** Generative AI has cut the marginal cost of every counted output, and its use is already broad and mostly ordinary polishing.
- **Evidence:**
  - Kobak et al. 2025: at least 13.5% of 2024 PubMed abstracts LLM-processed, up to ~40% in subgroups; ~15M abstracts (AB).
  - Liang et al. 2025: up to 22% of CS abstract sentences LLM-modified, Jan 2020–Sep 2024; 1,121,912 papers (AB).
  - Liang et al. 2024: 6.5–16.9% of review text at four AI conferences substantially LLM-modified (AB; conference paper).
- **Counter → answer:**
  - *Counter:* "LLM-modified" mostly means polishing; detectors work at population level and are biased against non-native writers (Liang et al. 2023).

**¶2.2 Signal 1, writing: honest use breaks it. [KEEP-3800] The core P1 paragraph.**

### Box 2. How this hypothesis could be tested (~350 words; draft text, revised from G3 §5). [KEEP-3800] (≈250 in the short versions: drop the measurement sentence and one regime)

> **Box 2 | How this hypothesis could be tested**
>
> The hypothesis is that metric incentives channel generative AI into exploitation: scientists use it to do faster what they already know how to do. Others now state it (Huang et al. 2026); no one has tested it. Hao et al. (2026) cannot settle it. Their "AI" is mostly machine learning used as a method; their contraction compares AI papers with other papers rather than following scientists; and in their data, data availability, not incentives, best predicts where AI concentrates.
>
> A discriminating test is a triple difference. Within scientists, compare exploration before and after November 2022 by intensity of AI use, and ask whether the effect is larger where counting is harsher: before versus after a first last-author paper; under evaluation regimes that count publications versus those that do not; and under short grant cycles versus long-horizon funding of the kind that encourages exploration (Azoulay et al. 2011). If incentives drive narrowing, AI users should narrow more where counting is harsher. If AI simply goes where the data are, the effect should track data availability instead. Because incentive intensity can itself correlate with how data-rich a field is, comparisons must be made within fields and topics, with data availability measured and interacted rather than assumed away.
>
> Exploration should be measured three ways: distance from a scientist's own earlier work, novelty against the field, and diversity of the whole system, per paper and per author-year. Reference-based measures should come first, because AI polishing makes abstracts alike and so mimics narrowing. Adoption must be measured independently of output (Renault et al. 2026). Detectors misclassify non-native writers, and arXiv's 2025–26 rules change who appears in the data.
>
> The hypothesis is falsifiable. It is weakened if AI users move further from their earlier work than comparable non-users, as early evidence suggests (Zheng et al. 2026; Kusumegi et al. 2025), or if the effect does not vary with incentive intensity. The data are open (OpenAlex; Semantic Scholar). [AUTHOR: confirm] I plan to run this test as a preregistered study.

### Figure 1. Conceptual diagram: scarcity → signals → gatekeeping
- **Panel a, before AI.**
  - Three costly inputs: writing effort (papers; grants, 34 days per proposal, Herbert et al.); reviewer time; years of mastery.
  - These make outputs scarce. Each scarce output signals its cost: a paper signals effort, a review an expert's time, a grant commitment, a synthesis mastery.
  - Gatekeepers (journals, panels, hiring) read the signals.
- **Panel b, general AI use, including legitimate use.**
  - Input costs collapse; outputs become abundant; the signals stop carrying information.
  - Gatekeepers are overloaded. Use the three anchors from Table 1: NIH ">40 applications in one round"; arXiv 40,363 a month; ICLR >62,000.
- **Panel c, two responses.**
  - **Rationing:** caps restore the scarcity of outputs by decree. The signal is not restored, and slots are sometimes allocated by prior counts (ICLR, AISTATS).
  - **Reform:** change what is counted. Count less and read more; price review; use costly signals AI cannot fake cheaply; reward exploration; build open infrastructure.
  - Dashed third arrow: fallback to prestige proxies (Blekhman 2026).

## 11. Known weaknesses of the argument as outlined

1. **"Legitimacy is irrelevant" rests on indirect evidence, and the institutional evidence points partly the other way.**
   - The academic support is thin: one *Science* study whose design is contested (Kusumegi; Renault et al.), non-academic labour markets (Galdin & Silbert; Cowgill) and detector-based prevalence.
   - The "mastery time" signal is a conceptual claim, with no evidence that gatekeepers relied on it.
   - The triggers for the caps are a minority of heavy submitters: NIH's ">40 applications"; arXiv's "relatively small proportion of authors". That reads as tail misuse as much as legitimate use.
2. **The narrowing story (§4–§5) is the weakest empirical link.**
   - The hypothesis is already published (Huang et al.) and untested.
   - The best within-author data point the other way (Zheng et al.), and most generative-era support is preprints.
   - Hao et al. study AI as a method, cross-sectionally, and their own analysis favours the rival (data availability).
   - The test only *discriminates* if within-field designs break the link between incentives and data richness.
   - Nothing yet links chat-data concentration to scientific narrowing (the external driver).
3. **The rationing reading risks over-generalising, and the remedy is thin and borrowed.**
   - arXiv calls its cap a "stopgap", and moderators are volunteers under real overload.
   - Reforms that change what counts do exist: CoARA, Utrecht, China 2020/2026.
   - The conference rows rest on one news article.
   - The alternatives (pricing, counting less, costly signals) come from Fourie, Lopez-Lira & Seyfi and Hansen and are untested, so the essay is stronger on diagnosis than on prescription.
- **Also:** the exhibit depends on time-and-cost figures that do not exist yet. A cheap *essay* does not show that research papers are cheap.
