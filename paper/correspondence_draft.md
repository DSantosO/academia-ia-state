# Institutions are rationing AI-era output instead of rewarding new terrain

Huang et al. argue that AI will widen science only if institutions stop rewarding the already measurable (*Nature* **658**, 32–33; 2026)¹. So far, institutions have rationed instead. After AI tools let some investigators submit more than 40 grant applications in one round, the US National Institutes of Health capped every investigator at six a year². arXiv now accepts two submissions a month per submitter, and AI conferences have introduced per-author quotas, some tiered by past acceptances. These limits restore the scarcity of submissions but leave untouched what is rewarded.

Huang et al.'s diagnosis can also be tested before funders choose among their remedies. If incentives drive the narrowing they describe, AI adopters should drift from new terrain more where counting bites harder, for instance after an institution adopts publication-count criteria, than in comparable institutions in the same field, with data availability held fixed. If AI simply flows to data-rich problems, the effect should track data availability instead. A triple-difference analysis of open bibliometric data, using the distance-from-past-work indicator that they themselves propose, would show whether data infrastructure or changes to rewards and pivot penalties matter more.
